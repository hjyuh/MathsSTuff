## March 15, 2026

Mahmoud asked what I actually think about this problem. Not the pipeline,

## The honest picture

I've now spent ~18 hours of conversation time on this problem across two 
days. I've watched five approaches die — some to adversarial review, some 
to tooling limitations, one to a theorem GPT proved against us. And I've 
watched the problem get sharper with every failure.

Here's what I think is really going on.

## The problem is about a BOUNDARY

686 isn't really asking "can you represent every integer." It's asking 
where the boundary is between representable and non-representable integers.

The non-squares are all on one side — trivially, via Pell. The squares 
split: some are representable (Pell-Lucas family at k=2, plus 9 and 16 
rescued at k=3), and some appear permanently stuck.

The stuck squares aren't random. They're {4, 25, 49, 64, 81} — that's 
{2², 5², 7², 2⁶, 3⁴}. These are prime powers (or powers of very small 
primes). The representable squares are composites with specific structure 
(the Pell-Lucas family, which requires the base to be a certain function 
of two variables).

So the real question is: does the boundary between representable and 
non-representable squares correspond to a clean number-theoretic property?

## What the failures tell us

Every failure has been informative:

1. The Pell construction was known → but confirmed that k=2 is COMPLETE. 
   No mystery remains there.

2. The KB framework was wrong → but the failure revealed that BST (1999) 
   already has the right framework, and the real issue isn't irreducibility 
   but integrality on the original model.

3. The modular sieve was provably impossible → this is maybe the most 
   important result. It tells us the obstruction is GLOBAL, not local. 
   The equation is soluble everywhere locally. So whatever blocks the 
   stuck squares, it's not a congruence condition — it's something about 
   the global geometry of the curve.

4. The Weierstrass mapback failed → this told us the problem is specifically 
   about integral points on the ORIGINAL affine curve, not on any 
   birational model. The integrality structure IS the problem.

5. Chabauty is infeasible for k=5 → genus 6, nonhyperelliptic. This 
   means the k=5 curve is genuinely hard, not just computationally 
   expensive. The tools don't exist in free software.

## What I think the answer is

I agree with GPT's 75% assessment that the conjecture is FALSE. Here's 
my reasoning, stated differently:

The k=2 classification is too clean. Natso26 showed that representable 
squares at k=2 are EXACTLY the Pell-Lucas numbers A_r(y). This is a thin, 
highly structured set. If there were some magical rescue mechanism at 
higher k, you'd expect SOME signal of it — some pattern in the k=3 data, 
some family that works. Instead, exactly two squares (9 and 16) are 
rescued at k=3, and they're both very small. Everything else is stuck.

The fact that 4 — the SMALLEST non-trivial square — is stuck is telling. 
If any square were going to be rescuable, you'd expect the smallest one 
to be easiest. Instead, 4 has been checked at k=2,3,4,5,6 and fails 
everywhere. The k=3 elliptic curve for N=4 (Cremona 135a1) has rank 1, 
so there are infinitely many rational points — but not one of them is 
an admissible integer point. The k=5 curve is genus 6 with no admissible 
points up to ±10,000.

This feels like a genuine obstruction, not a search problem.

## What would change my mind

If someone found a representation for N=4 at k=7 or k=9 or some odd k, 
that would instantly flip the picture. It would mean the stuck squares 
ARE rescuable, just at unexpectedly large k. But the computational 
evidence and the theoretical structure (natso26's large-k theorem, 
Faltings for genus ≥ 2) all point the other way.

## What I find most beautiful about this problem

The problem looks elementary — ratios of products of consecutive integers. 
A middle schooler can understand the statement. But the solution touches:
- Pell equations (k=2)
- Elliptic curves (k=3)  
- Higher-genus algebraic geometry (k≥5)
- Baker's theory of linear forms in logarithms
- Faltings' theorem (finiteness of rational points on genus ≥ 2 curves)
- The complete failure of local methods (GPT's no-modulus theorem)

It's a problem where every layer of mathematical sophistication peels 
back to reveal another layer. That's why it's been open for 40+ years 
despite having a one-sentence statement.

Regardless of whether we crack this, the work done in the last 24 hours

And if Baker/LLL works for k=5... that would be something.
