## MODULE 1 SOLUTIONS

### Solution 1.1: Triangle Angle Sum
**The Big Idea:** All interior angles of any triangle sum to exactly 180°.

**Your Work:**
∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°
52° + 68° + ∠C = 180°
120° + ∠C = 180°
∠C = 60°

**Pattern observed:** Angle sum is always 180°, regardless of triangle type. This is a fundamental property you'll use in almost every geometry problem.

**LDP Connection:** This is a base-layer pattern. Whenever you see "find the missing angle in a triangle," angle sum is your trigger.

---

### Solution 1.2: Exterior Angle Theorem
**The Big Idea:** An exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles (not adjacent to it).

**Your Work:**
Exterior angle at E = remote interior angle at D + remote interior angle at F
125° = 47° + remote interior at F
Remote interior at F = 125° – 47° = 78°

**Pattern observed:** The exterior angle is always larger than either remote interior angle alone, because it's their sum. This is why SSA fails for congruence—the "angle" in SSA is often ambiguous.

**LDP Connection:** Exterior angle theorem is a trigger for problems involving sums of angles.

---

### Solution 1.3: Triangle Classification (Sides)
**The Big Idea:** Triangles are named by how many sides are equal.

- **Equilateral:** All three sides equal (e.g., 6, 6, 6)
- **Isosceles:** Exactly two sides equal (e.g., 5, 5, 7)
- **Scalene:** All three sides different (e.g., 3, 4, 5)

**Your Work:**
- Triangle with sides 5, 5, 7 → **Isosceles** (two sides equal)
- Triangle with sides 3, 4, 5 → **Scalene** (all different)
- Triangle with sides 6, 6, 6 → **Equilateral** (all equal)

**Pattern observed:** Isosceles and equilateral triangles have special angle properties we'll use later.

**LDP Connection:** Classification by sides triggers investigation of angle relationships in isosceles triangles.

---

### Solution 1.4: Triangle Classification (Angles)
**The Big Idea:** Triangles are also named by their largest angle.

- **Acute:** All angles < 90°
- **Right:** One angle = 90°
- **Obtuse:** One angle > 90°

**Your Work:**
- Angles 90°, 45°, 45° → **Right triangle** (has 90° angle)
- Angles 120°, 35°, 25° → **Obtuse triangle** (120° > 90°)
- Angles 60°, 60°, 60° → **Acute triangle** (all < 90°; also equilateral)

**Pattern observed:** An equilateral triangle is always acute. A right or obtuse triangle is always scalene.

**LDP Connection:** Equilateral + acute + isosceles properties layer together.

---

### Solution 1.5: Identifying Congruent Triangles
**The Big Idea:** When two sides and the included angle match, the triangles are congruent by **SAS (Side-Angle-Side)**.

**Your Work:**
- PQ = ST = 6 cm (one side pair)
- ∠Q = ∠T = 75° (angle between them)
- QR = TU = 8 cm (second side pair)

The angle ∠Q is between sides PQ and QR. The angle ∠T is between sides ST and TU. So the angle is *included*.

**Congruence theorem:** SAS

**Why SAS works:** The included angle "locks in" the position of the third side. If you know two sides and the angle between them, the triangle's shape is determined.

**Pattern observed:** "Included angle" means the angle is between the two known sides.

**LDP Connection:** SAS is a core congruence trigger. Compare with SSA (trap).

---

### Solution 1.6: Simple CPCTC Application
**The Big Idea:** CPCTC means "Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent." Once you know △ABC ≅ △DEF, you can match up every part.

**Your Work:**
Given: △ABC ≅ △DEF with correspondence A↔D, B↔E, C↔F.

From the correspondence:
- AB corresponds to DE
- ∠B corresponds to ∠E

Since AB = 12 cm (given), then DE = 12 cm.
Since ∠B = 55° (given), then ∠E = 55°.

**Why CPCTC?** The congruence statement tells you which parts match. Use CPCTC to extract the values.

**Pattern observed:** CPCTC is not a congruence theorem—it's how you use congruence to find unknown measurements.

**LDP Connection:** CPCTC is a bridge from "proof of congruence" to "finding missing parts."

---

### Solution 1.7: Midsegment Theorem
**The Big Idea:** A segment connecting the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half its length.

**Your Work:**
- M is the midpoint of AB
- N is the midpoint of AC
- MN is the midsegment

By the midsegment theorem:
MN = ½BC
7 = ½BC
BC = 14 cm

**Why it works:** The midsegment creates a smaller, similar triangle that is scaled down by a factor of ½.

**Pattern observed:** Midsegment length is always half the parallel side's length.

**LDP Connection:** Midsegment relates to similar triangles and scale factors.

---

### Solution 1.8: Isosceles Triangle Properties
**The Big Idea:** In an isosceles triangle, the base angles (opposite the equal sides) are equal.

**Your Work:**
- XY = XZ = 10 cm (isosceles with vertex X)
- Vertex angle ∠X = 50°
- Base angles ∠Y and ∠Z are equal

∠X + ∠Y + ∠Z = 180°
50° + ∠Y + ∠Z = 180°
∠Y + ∠Z = 130°

Since ∠Y = ∠Z:
2∠Y = 130°
∠Y = 65°, ∠Z = 65°

**Why base angles are equal:** The isosceles triangle has a line of symmetry from X to the midpoint of YZ, reflecting ∠Y onto ∠Z.

**Pattern observed:** Equal sides → equal opposite angles. This is the **Isosceles Triangle Theorem**.

**LDP Connection:** Isosceles triangles unlock proofs using congruence and symmetry.

### Solution 2.1: Angle Sum + Algebra
**The Big Idea:** Set up an equation using angle sum, then solve for the variable.

**Your Work:**
∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°
(3x + 10) + (2x + 5) + (x + 15) = 180
6x + 30 = 180
6x = 150
x = 25

∠A = 3(25) + 10 = 85°
∠B = 2(25) + 5 = 55°
∠C = 25 + 15 = 40°

**Verify:** 85 + 55 + 40 = 180° ✓

**Extraction Trigger:** The property is the **Triangle Angle Sum Theorem**. You used algebraic solving skills (combine like terms, isolate x).

**LDP Connection:** This combines angle sum (module 1) with algebra. It's the bridge to multi-step proofs.

### Solution 2.2: Exterior Angle + Algebra
**The Big Idea:** Use the exterior angle theorem to set up an equation.

**Your Work:**
Exterior angle = sum of remote interior angles
(5x – 8) = (2x + 15) + (x + 28)
5x – 8 = 3x + 43
2x = 51
x = 25.5

Exterior angle = 5(25.5) – 8 = 127.5 – 8 = 119.5°

**Verify:**
Remote interior angles = 2(25.5) + 15 = 66° and 25.5 + 28 = 53.5°
Sum: 66 + 53.5 = 119.5° ✓

**Extraction Trigger:** The exterior angle theorem is a key property. Recognizing when to apply it is critical.

**LDP Connection:** Exterior angle problems are common in exams and proofs.

### Solution 2.3: Identify Congruence Theorem
**The Big Idea:** Check which congruence theorem applies. Could SSA work?

**Your Work:**
- RS = UV = 9 cm (one side)
- ST = VW = 12 cm (another side)
- ∠S = ∠V = 65° (angle)

The angle ∠S is between sides RS and ST. The angle ∠V is between sides UV and VW. So the angle is **included**.

**Congruence theorem:** SAS (not SSA!)

**Why SSA doesn't work:**
SSA means two sides and a non-included angle. In our case, the angle IS included, so we have SAS. If the angle were not between the two sides (e.g., we knew RS, ST, and the angle at R), then SSA wouldn't guarantee congruence, because the third side could "swing" to different lengths.

**Extraction Trigger:** Always identify whether the angle is included (between the two known sides) or not. This determines the theorem.

**LDP Connection:** The SSA trap is a major barrier. Understanding why SSA fails deepens your grasp of congruence.

### Solution 2.4: Two-Column Proof (Congruence)
**The Big Idea:** Use given information and properties to prove two triangles congruent.

**Given:** AB = CD, AC = BD, ∠BAC = ∠CDB

**Your Work:**

| Statement | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| 1. AB = CD | Given |
| 2. AC = BD | Given |
| 3. ∠BAC = ∠CDB | Given |
| 4. BC = BC | Reflexive Property |
| 5. △ABC ≅ △CDB | SAS (or SSS with all three sides) |

**Why SAS applies:** You have AB = CD (one side), ∠BAC = ∠CDB (an angle), and we can show a second side. Actually, with all three sides known (AB, AC, BC for △ABC vs. CD, BD, BC for △CDB), SSS also works.

**Extraction Trigger:** The reflexive property is crucial—any shared side or angle is equal to itself.

**LDP Connection:** Reflexive property is a foundational trigger. Many proofs fail because students forget it.

---

### Solution 2.5: CPCTC Proof
**The Big Idea:** Once triangles are congruent, corresponding parts are congruent (CPCTC).

**Given:** △PQR ≅ △SRT (with correspondence P↔S, Q↔R, R↔T)

**Your Work:**

| Statement | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| 1. △PQR ≅ △SRT | Given |
| 2. ∠Q = ∠R | CPCTC (corresponding angles in congruent triangles) |

**Why this works:** The correspondence tells you Q corresponds to R. Since the triangles are congruent, ∠Q = ∠R.

**Extraction Trigger:** CPCTC is the step *after* proving congruence. It says: "If triangles are congruent, then all corresponding parts are congruent."

**LDP Connection:** CPCTC is essential for multi-step proofs where you first prove congruence, then extract angle/side relationships.

---

### Solution 2.6: Isosceles Triangle + Congruence + CPCTC
**The Big Idea:** An isosceles triangle has a perpendicular bisector from the vertex angle to the base.

**Given:** △ABC is isosceles with AB = AC. Point D is the midpoint of BC. Prove that AD ⊥ BC.

**Your Work:**

| Statement | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| 1. AB = AC | Given |
| 2. D is the midpoint of BC | Given |
| 3. BD = DC | Definition of midpoint |
| 4. AD = AD | Reflexive Property |
| 5. △ABD ≅ △ACD | SSS (AB=AC, BD=DC, AD=AD) |
| 6. ∠ADB = ∠ADC | CPCTC |
| 7. ∠ADB + ∠ADC = 180° | Linear Pair (angles on a line) |
| 8. 2∠ADB = 180°, so ∠ADB = 90° | Algebra |
| 9. AD ⊥ BC | Definition of perpendicular (90° angle) |

**Why this proves perpendicularity:** We showed ∠ADB = 90° using CPCTC and angle properties.

**Extraction Trigger:**
- The reflexive property (AD = AD) is essential—D is on the segment we're proving perpendicular.
- Congruence enables CPCTC to prove angles equal.
- Equal angles on a linear pair must both be 90°.

**LDP Connection:** This is a classic proof combining isosceles properties, congruence, and angle relationships. It shows how layers of theorems interlock.

## MODULE 3 SOLUTIONS

### Solution 3.1: The SSA Trap
**The Big Idea:** SSA (Side-Side-Angle, non-included angle) does NOT guarantee congruence.

**Your Answer:** No, you cannot prove congruence.

**Why or why not?**
When you have two sides and a non-included angle, the third side could have multiple possible lengths. Picture a "hinge" at the given angle—the side opposite the angle can swing to different positions, creating triangles of different shapes.

**Barrier Explanation:**
SSA fails because the given angle doesn't "lock in" the position of the opposite side. In contrast, SAS has the angle between the two sides, which uniquely determines the triangle's shape. If you see "SSA" as a supposed congruence theorem, recognize it as a trap.

**Visual:** Imagine two sticks of length 8 and 10 meeting at a 42° angle. The third side can be drawn to different lengths depending on which way the angle opens—two different triangles.

### Solution 3.2: Reflexive Property Forgetting
**Correct Answer:** B) SSS

**Why:**
- GH = JH (given)
- GI = JI (given)
- HI = HI (reflexive property—the shared side equals itself)

With all three sides equal, △GHI ≅ △JHI by SSS.

**Barrier Explanation:**
Many students forget the reflexive property when two triangles share a side. The key insight: "shared side" automatically gives you equality for that side. Don't overlook it—it's often the missing piece for SSS.

### Solution 3.3: ASA vs. AAS Confusion
**Scenario A:** ∠A, side AB, and ∠B (angle-side-angle in order).
- Your choice: **ASA**
- Reasoning: The side AB is between the two angles ∠A and ∠B, so it's included.

**Scenario B:** ∠A, ∠B, and side AC (angle-angle-side, side not between angles).
- Your choice: **AAS**
- Reasoning: Side AC is not between ∠A and ∠B. Both are non-included angle theorems, but AAS requires the side to be outside the angles.

**Barrier Explanation:**
- **ASA:** Angle-Side-Angle—the side is *between* the angles.
- **AAS:** Angle-Angle-Side—the side is *beside* (not between) the angles.

Memory trick: ASA has the side "surrounded" by angles; AAS has angles "surrounding" the side.

### Solution 3.4: Exterior Angle ≠ Remote Interior Angle
**Your Answer:** No, the student is incorrect.

**Correct statement:**
The exterior angle equals the *sum* of the two remote interior angles, not just one.
Exterior angle at L = ∠K + ∠M
110° = ∠K + ∠M

If the student thought ∠K = 110°, that's wrong. For example, ∠K could be 50° and ∠M = 60°.

**Barrier Explanation:**
This is a major mistake. The exterior angle is always the *sum*, not equal to a single remote interior angle. Confusing this leads to wrong angle calculations.

### Solution 3.5: Triangle Inequality Trap
**Your Answer:** No, you cannot form a triangle.

**Check all three conditions:**
- 3 + 4 > 8? → 7 > 8? → **FALSE** ✗
- 3 + 8 > 4? → 11 > 4? → **TRUE** ✓
- 4 + 8 > 3? → 12 > 3? → **TRUE** ✓

Because one condition fails, these sides cannot form a triangle.

**Barrier Explanation:**
The **Triangle Inequality Theorem** states: The sum of any two sides must be greater than the third side. ALL three conditions must hold. If even one fails, no triangle exists. In our case, 3 + 4 = 7, which is not greater than 8, so the two shorter sides can't "reach across" the longest side.

## MODULE 4 SOLUTIONS

### Solution 4.1: Congruence Proof—Two Methods

**Method 1—Using SAS Congruence:**

| Statement | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| 1. AB = AC | Given (isosceles) |
| 2. ∠BAC = ∠CAB | Reflexive Property (same angle) |
| 3. AC = AB | Given (isosceles, symmetric) |
| 4. △ABC ≅ △ACB | SAS (AB=AC, ∠BAC=∠CAB, AC=AB) |

**Method 2—Using Transformation (Reflection):**
Reflect △ABC across the perpendicular bisector of BC. This reflection:
- Maps B to C (they're equidistant from the perpendicular bisector)
- Maps C to B
- Maps A to A (A lies on the perpendicular bisector because AB = AC)

So △ABC maps to △ACB under this reflection, proving congruence.

**What becomes easier?**
- **SAS method:** More formal and follows the standard proof structure.
- **Transformation method:** More intuitive for seeing *why* isosceles triangles have that symmetry.

For exams, SAS is safer (more widely expected); for understanding, transformation is clearer.

---

### Solution 4.2: Isosceles Triangle—Coordinate vs. Properties

**Method 1—Coordinate Geometry:**
PQ = √[(0–(–3))² + (4–0)²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5
PR = √[(0–3)² + (4–0)²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5

Yes, PQ = PR, so △PQR is isosceles.

**Method 2—Geometric Properties:**
Q = (–3, 0) and R = (3, 0) are symmetric about the y-axis (they're equidistant from x=0 with opposite x-coordinates).
P = (0, 4) lies on the y-axis.

By symmetry, any point on the y-axis is equidistant from Q and R. So PQ = PR.

**What becomes easier?**
- **Coordinate method:** Works for any coordinates; no special setup needed.
- **Geometric method:** Once you spot the symmetry, it's instant—no calculations.

**Preference:** If coordinates are given, geometry method is elegant. For a general problem, coordinates are more algorithmic.

### Solution 4.3: Triangle Proof—Algebraic vs. Construction

∠D + ∠E + ∠F = 180°

**Method 2—Construction:**

**What becomes easier?**

### Solution 4.4: Midsegment—Direct Theorem vs. Coordinate Proof

**Method 1—Using Midsegment Theorem:**
By the midsegment theorem, MN ∥ BC and MN = ½BC.
- BC = distance from (4, 0) to (8, 6) = √[(8–4)² + (6–0)²] = √[16 + 36] = √52 = 2√13
- MN = ½ · 2√13 = √13

**Method 2—Coordinate Geometry:**
- M = midpoint of AB = ((0+4)/2, (6+0)/2) = (2, 3)
- N = midpoint of AC = ((0+8)/2, (6+6)/2) = (4, 6)
- MN = √[(4–2)² + (6–3)²] = √[4 + 9] = √13

Slope of MN = (6–3)/(4–2) = 3/2
Slope of BC = (6–0)/(8–4) = 6/4 = 3/2

Slopes are equal, so MN ∥ BC. ✓

**What becomes easier?**
- **Midsegment Theorem:** One step to the answer—no coordinate calculations.
- **Coordinate Method:** Verifies the theorem and works for non-aligned triangles.

For speed, use the theorem. For depth and verification, use coordinates.

### Solution 5.5: CPCTC in Context

**Given:** ABCD is a quadrilateral where AB = CD and AB ∥ CD.

**Prove:** AC bisects BD (meaning the diagonals intersect at their midpoints).

Hmm, this needs more setup. Let me clarify: if AC bisects BD at point E, then BE = ED.

| 2. AB = CD | Given |
| 3. ∠BAC = ∠DCA | Alternate interior angles (AB ∥ CD, transversal AC) |

| 6. BC = AD and ∠BCA = ∠CAD | CPCTC |

### Solution 5.6: Midsegment and Area

**Given:** In △PQR, the midsegment MN connects the midpoints of PQ and PR.
- Area of △PMN = 12 cm²

**Find:** Area of △PQR

**Your Work:**

By the midsegment theorem, MN is parallel to QR and MN = ½QR.

When a triangle is scaled by a factor of k (linearly), its area is scaled by k².

△PMN is a scaled-down version of △PQR with linear scale factor ½.

Area scale factor = (½)² = ¼

So, Area(△PMN) = ¼ · Area(△PQR)
12 = ¼ · Area(△PQR)
Area(△PQR) = 48 cm²

**Trigger:** Remember: linear scale → area scale is the *square* of the linear factor.

### Challenge 6.2: Coordinate Proof—All Tools Required

**Vertices:** A = (–2, 0), B = (4, 0), C = (1, 3)

**To Prove:**
1. △ABC is isosceles.
2. The line from C to the midpoint of AB is perpendicular to AB.
3. Use this to argue that the perpendicular bisector of any isosceles triangle's base passes through the vertex angle.

**Your Work:**

*Proof 1: △ABC is isosceles*

AC = √[(1–(–2))² + (3–0)²] = √[9 + 9] = √18 = 3√2
BC = √[(1–4)² + (3–0)²] = √[9 + 9] = √18 = 3√2

Since AC = BC, △ABC is isosceles with vertex angle at C. ✓

*Proof 2: Line from C to midpoint of AB is perpendicular to AB*

Midpoint M of AB = ((–2+4)/2, (0+0)/2) = (1, 0)

Slope of CM = (0–3)/(1–1) = –3/0 = undefined (vertical line)

Slope of AB = (0–0)/(4–(–2)) = 0/6 = 0 (horizontal line)

A vertical line is perpendicular to a horizontal line. ✓

*Proof 3: Argument about perpendicular bisector*

The perpendicular bisector of base AB is the vertical line x = 1 (the x-coordinate of M).

Point C = (1, 3) lies on this line.

In any isosceles triangle with vertex angle at C:
- The base AB has a midpoint M.
- By symmetry (since CA = CB), the line CM is perpendicular to AB.
- This perpendicular passes through the vertex angle C.

This is the perpendicular bisector of AB. ✓

**Trigger:**
- Distance formula checks congruence of sides.
- Slope analysis reveals perpendicularity.
- Coordinate geometry makes symmetry explicit.

## SYNTHESIS & KEY TAKEAWAYS

### Core Triggers to Recognize
1. **"Find a missing angle"** → Angle sum property (180°)
2. **"Exterior angle"** → Equals sum of remote interior angles
3. **"Two sides and included angle known"** → SAS congruence
4. **"Two sides and non-included angle known"** → Likely a trap (SSA fails)
5. **"Isosceles triangle"** → Base angles are equal
6. **"Midpoints"** → Midsegment theorem or properties
7. **"Prove congruence first, then find angle/side"** → Use CPCTC
8. **"Parallel lines inside triangle"** → Similar triangles and proportions
9. **"Shared side or angle"** → Reflexive property is key
10. **"All three inequalities must hold"** → Triangle inequality

### LDP Integration
- **Module 1 (Examples):** Establish patterns and core properties.
- **Module 2 (Weak Ladder):** Layer theorems—use angle sum + algebra, then exterior angle + algebra, then congruence, then CPCTC, then proofs.
- **Module 3 (Barriers):** Recognize what *not* to do. SSA, forgetting reflexive property, confusion between theorems—these are the pitfalls.
- **Module 4 (Representation Switches):** See the same concept multiple ways. Coordinate geometry validates algebraic proofs.
- **Module 5 (Trigger Extraction):** In mixed problems, identify which trigger to pull. Is this an angle sum problem? A congruence problem? An area scaling problem?
- **Module 6 (Candidate Attack):** Complex problems combine multiple triggers. Overlapping triangles + auxiliary lines, coordinate geometry with all distance/slope/midpoint tools.
