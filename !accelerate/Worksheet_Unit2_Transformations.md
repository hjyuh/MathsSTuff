# INTUITIVE EXPLANATIONS & FULL SOLUTIONS

## Module 1: Model Examples — Detailed Solutions

### Solution 1.1: Translation of a Point
**The Problem:** Translate $P(3, -2)$ by vector $\langle -4, 5 \rangle$.

**Explanation:**
A translation shifts every point by the same amount in the same direction. The vector $\langle -4, 5 \rangle$ tells us: move 4 units left (negative x-direction) and 5 units up (positive y-direction).

**Calculation:**
$$P' = P + \text{vector} = (3, -2) + \langle -4, 5 \rangle = (3 + (-4), -2 + 5) = (-1, 3)$$

**Pattern observed:** Translations use vector addition. The image point is always the same distance and direction from the pre-image.

**Why this matters:** Translations are rigid motions—they preserve distance and shape. This is the foundation for proving congruence.

---

### Solution 1.2: Reflection Over the x-Axis
**The Problem:** Reflect triangle $ABC$ with $A(1, 2)$, $B(4, 2)$, $C(4, 6)$ over the x-axis.

**Explanation:**
Reflection over the x-axis is a "flip" where every point moves to the opposite side of the x-axis, the same distance away. The coordinate rule is $(x, y) \to (x, -y)$: the x-coordinate stays the same, but the y-coordinate changes sign.

**Calculation:**
- $A(1, 2) \to A'(1, -2)$
- $B(4, 2) \to B'(4, -2)$
- $C(4, 6) \to C'(4, -6)$

**Congruence Check:**
- Distance $|AB| = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (2-2)^2} = 3$
- Distance $|A'B'| = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (-2-(-2))^2} = 3$ ✓

The image is congruent because reflection is a rigid motion.

**Pattern observed:** The x-axis acts as a "mirror." Points on one side map to points on the other, equidistant from the x-axis.

**Why this matters:** Reflections are rigid motions. Understanding the coordinate rules helps you apply them efficiently and predict the image without graphing.

---

### Solution 1.3: Reflection Over the y-Axis
**The Problem:** Reflect $Q(5, -3)$ over the y-axis.

**Explanation:**
Reflection over the y-axis flips the point across the y-axis (the vertical line $x = 0$). The coordinate rule is $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$: the x-coordinate changes sign, the y-coordinate stays the same.

**Calculation:**
$$Q' = (-5, -3)$$

**Coordinate Rule:** $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$

**Pattern observed:** The y-axis is the mirror. The image is the same distance from the y-axis as the pre-image, but on the opposite side.

---

### Solution 1.4: Reflection Over the Line $y = x$
**The Problem:** Reflect triangle $DEF$ with $D(1, 0)$, $E(3, 1)$, $F(2, 4)$ over the line $y = x$.

**Explanation:**
The line $y = x$ is a diagonal. Reflection over $y = x$ swaps the coordinates: $(x, y) \to (y, x)$. Think of it this way: the line $y = x$ is the "fold line." If you fold the plane along $y = x$, point $(x, y)$ lands on $(y, x)$.

**Calculation:**
- $D(1, 0) \to D'(0, 1)$
- $E(3, 1) \to E'(1, 3)$
- $F(2, 4) \to F'(4, 2)$

**Coordinate Rule:** $(x, y) \to (y, x)$

**Pattern observed:** Coordinates swap! This is one of the most elegant transformations because the rule is so simple.

**Why this matters:** Reflection over $y = x$ is used in inverse functions (the graph of $f^{-1}$ is the reflection of $f$ over $y = x$).

---

### Solution 1.5: Reflection Over the Line $y = -x$
**The Problem:** Reflect $R(2, 5)$ over the line $y = -x$.

**Explanation:**
The line $y = -x$ is a diagonal with negative slope. Reflection over $y = -x$ has the rule $(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$: both coordinates change sign, AND they swap.

**Calculation:**
$$R' = (-5, -2)$$

**Coordinate Rule:** $(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$

**Comparison to Solution 1.4:**
- Over $y = x$: $(x, y) \to (y, x)$ — swap only
- Over $y = -x$: $(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$ — negate both, then swap

**Pattern observed:** The difference between these two reflections is whether we negate first or swap first. Both approaches work algebraically, but the geometric picture is: fold along the diagonal line.

---

### Solution 1.6: Rotation 90° Counterclockwise
**The Problem:** Rotate $S(3, 4)$ by 90° CCW about the origin.

**Explanation:**
Rotation preserves distance from the center. The point $S(3, 4)$ is at distance $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ from the origin. After rotating 90° counterclockwise, it will still be 5 units away, but its angle will increase by 90°.

**Coordinate Rule:** $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$

**Calculation:**
$$S' = (-4, 3)$$

**Verification:**
- Distance from origin to $S$: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ ✓
- Distance from origin to $S'$: $\sqrt{(-4)^2 + 3^2} = 5$ ✓

**Pattern observed:** In a 90° CCW rotation, the new x-coordinate is the negative of the old y-coordinate, and the new y-coordinate is the old x-coordinate. Distances from the origin are preserved.

**Why this matters:** This coordinate rule is foundational for all rotations. Remember: CCW means counterclockwise (the direction a clock's hands move backward).

---

### Solution 1.7: Rotation 180° About the Origin
**The Problem:** Rotate quadrilateral $WXYZ$ with $W(1, 2)$, $X(4, 2)$, $Y(4, 5)$, $Z(1, 5)$ by 180°.

**Explanation:**
A 180° rotation "flips" the figure through the origin. Every point moves to the opposite side of the origin, the same distance away. The coordinate rule is $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$: both coordinates change sign.

**Calculation:**
- $W(1, 2) \to W'(-1, -2)$
- $X(4, 2) \to X'(-4, -2)$
- $Y(4, 5) \to Y'(-4, -5)$
- $Z(1, 5) \to Z'(-1, -5)$

**Shape Analysis:**
$WXYZ$ is a rectangle (with sides parallel to the axes). $W'X'Y'Z'$ is also a rectangle, congruent to the original. The image is the same shape because 180° rotation is a rigid motion.

**Pattern observed:** 180° rotation is equivalent to two reflections: reflect over the x-axis, then reflect over the y-axis (or vice versa).

---

### Solution 1.8: Dilation from the Origin
**The Problem:** Dilate triangle $GHI$ with $G(1, 1)$, $H(2, 1)$, $I(1, 3)$ from the origin with scale factor $k = 2$.

**Explanation:**
Dilation scales distances from the center. With scale factor $k = 2$, every point's distance from the origin is doubled. The coordinate rule is $(x, y) \to (2x, 2y)$.

**Calculation:**
- $G(1, 1) \to G'(2, 2)$
- $H(2, 1) \to H'(4, 2)$
- $I(1, 3) \to I'(2, 6)$

**Area Comparison:**
- Area of $GHI$: $\frac{1}{2} |1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 2 - 3 \cdot 2| = 1$ (using the shoelace formula)
- Area of $G'H'I'$: $\frac{1}{2} |2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 6 - 2 \cdot 2 - 2 \cdot 4 - 6 \cdot 2| = 4$

Wait, let me recalculate more carefully:
- Area of $GHI$ = $\frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 1$
- Area of $G'H'I'$ = $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 4$

The area is multiplied by $k^2 = 4$. This is a general rule: when you dilate by scale factor $k$, areas are multiplied by $k^2$.

**Pattern observed:** Dilation with scale factor $k$ multiplies all distances by $k$ and all areas by $k^2$. Unlike translations and rotations, dilation is NOT a rigid motion (unless $k = 1$).

**Why this matters:** Understanding that dilation is non-rigid helps you identify when you can use it to prove congruence (answer: never, unless combined with rigid motions).

- $(0, 0) \to (-0, 0) = (0, 0)$ ✓

- $(0, 4) \to (-4, 0)$ ✓

**Conclusion:** The transformation is a 90° counterclockwise rotation about the origin. This IS a rigid motion.

### Solution 1.10: Symmetry Recognition
**The Problem:** Square $ABCD$ with $A(1, 1)$, $B(3, 1)$, $C(3, 3)$, $D(1, 3)$. Find lines of symmetry and rotational symmetries.

**Explanation:**
A square has symmetry when a transformation maps it onto itself.

**Lines of Symmetry:**
1. $x = 2$ (vertical line through the center) — reflects $A \leftrightarrow B$ and $D \leftrightarrow C$
2. $y = 2$ (horizontal line through the center) — reflects $A \leftrightarrow D$ and $B \leftrightarrow C$

4. $y = 4 - x$ (other diagonal) — swaps $A \leftrightarrow C$, $B \leftrightarrow B$, $D \leftrightarrow D$

A square has 4 lines of symmetry.

**Rotational Symmetries:**
- 90° rotation about the center $(2, 2)$: $A \to B \to C \to D \to A$ ✓
- 180° rotation about the center: $A \leftrightarrow C$, $B \leftrightarrow D$ ✓
- 270° rotation about the center: $A \to D \to C \to B \to A$ ✓

A square has rotational symmetries at 90°, 180°, and 270°. (We don't count 0° or 360° separately.)

**Pattern observed:** A square has 4 lines of symmetry and 3 non-trivial rotational symmetries. Regular polygons with $n$ sides have $n$ lines of symmetry and rotational symmetries every $360°/n$.

### Solution 2.1: Single Transformation with Coordinate Rule
**The Problem:** Reflect $J(-3, 7)$ over the vertical line $x = 2$.

**Explanation:**

$(x, y) \to (2a - x, y)$

For $x = 2$: $(x, y) \to (4 - x, y)$

**Verification:** $(-3, 7) \to (4 - (-3), 7) = (7, 7)$ ✓

**Trigger:** I used the property that a vertical line is the perpendicular bisector of the segment $JJ'$ because I saw that $x = 2$ is a fixed vertical line. This will break when the line is not vertical or when the line passes through the point itself (then the point is fixed).

### Solution 2.2: Composition of Two Transformations
**The Problem:** Reflect $K(2, 3)$ over the y-axis, then translate the result by $\langle 3, -2 \rangle$.

**Explanation:**

$(x, y) \to (-x, y)$

**Step 2 — Translate by $\langle 3, -2 \rangle$:**
$(x, y) \to (x + 3, y - 2)$
$$K'(-2, 3) \to K''(-2 + 3, 3 - 2) = K''(1, 1)$$

**Final Answer:** $K'' = (1, 1)$

**Trigger:** I used sequential application of transformation rules because I saw that two transformations were applied "first...then," indicating an order. **The Problem:** Translate $M(1, 4)$ by $\langle 2, 3 \rangle$, then reflect the result over the x-axis.

**Explanation:**

**Step 1 — Translate by $\langle 2, 3 \rangle$:**

**Step 2 — Reflect over the x-axis:**
$$M'(3, 7) \to M''(3, -7)$$

### Solution 2.6: Complete Symmetry Analysis
**The Problem:** Regular hexagon centered at origin with one vertex at $(2, 0)$. Find all lines of symmetry and rotational symmetries.

## Module 3: Barrier Inventory — Common Mistakes (Clarified)

### Barrier 3.1: Rotation Direction Confusion — Solution
**The Trap:** Rotating $(3, 0)$ by 90° gives $(0, -3)$ instead of $(0, 3)$.

**What went wrong:**
The student applied a 90° **clockwise** rotation instead of counterclockwise.
- 90° CCW: $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$, so $(3, 0) \to (0, 3)$
- 90° CW: $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$, so $(3, 0) \to (0, -3)$

**Why is this a trap?**
Clockwise and counterclockwise look similar but produce opposite results. In standard coordinates, counterclockwise is the positive direction (angles increase CCW), but students often think "clockwise" because they're used to clock hands.

**Barrier Table:**
| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid It |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Confusing CW and CCW | "Clockwise" sounds intuitive (like a clock), but math uses CCW as positive | Remember: in standard coordinates, positive angles are measured CCW from the positive x-axis. Always check which direction the problem specifies. |
| Misapplying the coordinate rule | If you remember the rule wrong, all rotations will be wrong | Write out the rule: 90° CCW is $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$. Memorize this! |
| Not verifying the result | After computing, you don't check if the image makes sense | Verify: rotation preserves distance from the origin. Check: $\text{distance of } (3,0) = 3$ and distance of $(0, 3) = 3$. ✓ |

---

### Barrier 3.2: Composition Order Matters — Solution
**The Trap:** When you have "reflect over y-axis, then translate," applying translation first gives a different result.

**Example showing why order matters:**
Start with $P(2, 1)$.

Correct order (reflect, then translate by $\langle 3, 2 \rangle$):
- Reflect over y-axis: $(2, 1) \to (-2, 1)$
- Translate: $(-2, 1) \to (-2 + 3, 1 + 2) = (1, 3)$
- **Final:** $(1, 3)$

Wrong order (translate, then reflect):
- Translate by $\langle 3, 2 \rangle$: $(2, 1) \to (2 + 3, 1 + 2) = (5, 3)$
- Reflect over y-axis: $(5, 3) \to (-5, 3)$
- **Final:** $(-5, 3)$

These are different! $(1, 3) \ne (-5, 3)$

**Barrier Table:**
| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid It |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Applying transformations in wrong order | The problem statement says "first" and "then," but you might read it backwards | Underline "first" and "then." Apply in that exact order. Write intermediate results. |
| Forgetting to apply transformations step-by-step | You try to combine everything at once | Break compositions into steps. Never skip steps when learning. |
| Assuming compositions are commutative | In some cases (two translations), order doesn't matter, so you assume it never matters | Most compositions are NOT commutative. Always apply in the specified order unless you've proven order doesn't matter. |

### Barrier 3.3: Dilation is Not a Rigid Motion — Solution
**The Trap:** A student claims a dilation proves congruence.

**Why it's wrong:**
- Rigid motions preserve **all** distances.
- Dilation multiplies distances by the scale factor. If $k \ne 1$, distances are changed.
- Congruence requires that all side lengths are **exactly** the same, not scaled.

**Correct understanding:**
- A rigid motion **preserves** distances and angles.
- A dilation **scales** distances (by factor $k$) and **changes area** (by factor $k^2$).
- Congruence requires **rigid motion** transformations only.

**Barrier Table:**
| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid It |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Assuming dilation proves congruence | You might think "if the shape looks the same (just bigger), they're congruent" | Remember the definition: congruent means same shape AND same size. Dilation changes the size (unless $k=1$). |
| Confusing similarity and congruence | Similar figures have the same shape but different sizes (connected by dilation). Congruent figures have the same shape and size (connected by rigid motions). | Congruence = identical. Similarity = same shape, possibly different size. |
| Forgetting that $k=1$ is special | Dilation with scale factor 1 is actually the identity (no change) — a rigid motion | Only dilation with $k=1$ is rigid. All other dilations are non-rigid. |

### Barrier 3.4: Reflection Over $y = x$ vs $y = -x$ — Solution
**The Trap:** Confusing the coordinate rules for these two reflections.

**Clarifying by example:**
Reflect $(3, 1)$ over $y = x$: $(3, 1) \to (1, 3)$
Reflect $(3, 1)$ over $y = -x$: $(3, 1) \to (-1, -3)$

**The Rules:**
- Over $y = x$: $(x, y) \to (y, x)$ — swap coordinates
- Over $y = -x$: $(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$ — negate both, then swap (or equivalently, swap then negate)

**Memory Trick:**
The line $y = x$ goes through the first and third quadrants at a 45° angle (positive slope). Reflection over this line swaps $x$ and $y$.

The line $y = -x$ goes through the second and fourth quadrants at a -45° angle (negative slope). Reflection over this line swaps and negates both coordinates.

**Barrier Table:**
| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid It |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Mixing up the two reflection rules | Both involve swapping, so you might confuse which also negates | Over $y=x$: just swap. Over $y=-x$: negate both, then swap. Test with a point like $(1, 0)$: it goes to $(0, 1)$ over $y=x$ and $(0, -1)$ over $y=-x$. |
| Forgetting the negation in $y = -x$ reflection | You remember "swap," but forget the negation part | The negation comes from the negative slope of the line $y = -x$. Think: negative line → negate coordinates. |
| Applying the wrong rule to the wrong line | You use $(x, y) \to (-y, -x)$ for both, or $(x, y) \to (y, x)$ for both | Write the rules separately and verify with a test point. |

### Barrier 3.5: Mixing Up 90° vs 270° Rotations — Solution
**The Trap:** Confusing the coordinate rules for 90° and 270° rotations.

**Test your understanding:**
- 90° CCW rotation of $(1, 0)$: $(1, 0) \to (0, 1)$ [using $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$]
- 180° rotation of $(1, 0)$: $(1, 0) \to (-1, 0)$ [using $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$]
- 270° CCW rotation of $(1, 0)$: $(1, 0) \to (0, -1)$ [using $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$]

**Equivalent rotations:**
- 270° CCW = 90° CW (they're the same rotation, just described differently)
- 270° CCW = 90° CCW + 180° + another 0° — no, that's wrong. 270° CCW = 3 × 90° CCW

**Coordinate Rules for All Rotations:**
- 90° CCW: $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$
- 180°: $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$
- 270° CCW: $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$ [same as 90° CW]

**Pattern:** Each 90° step rotates further. The pattern cycles: $(x, y) \to (-y, x) \to (-x, -y) \to (y, -x) \to (x, y)$

**Barrier Table:**
| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid It |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Confusing 90° and 270° rules | Both involve coordinates changing sign and swapping, so it's easy to mix | Memorize the pattern: 90° CCW is $(-y, x)$. Then 270° CCW = 90° CW = $(y, -x)$. Or, 270° = 3 × 90°, so apply the 90° rule three times. |
| Forgetting that 270° CCW = 90° CW | These are the same rotation. Saying "270° counterclockwise" is awkward; "90° clockwise" is clearer. | If the problem says 270° CCW, convert it mentally: "That's 90° clockwise," which is easier to remember as $(y, -x)$. |
| Applying the rule in the wrong direction | You might compute 270° CCW when the problem asks for 90° CCW. | Read the problem carefully. Underline the degree measure and direction. |

## Module 4: Representation Switches — Detailed Solutions

### Solution 4.1: Verbal → Coordinate Rule → Geometric Motion
**Setup:** Describe a 180° rotation of $(2, 3)$ about the origin.

**Method A — Verbal:**

$(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$

### Solution 4.3: Proving Congruence — Two Approaches
**Setup:** Prove $\triangle PQR$ with $P(1, 1)$, $Q(4, 1)$, $R(1, 5)$ is congruent to $\triangle P'Q'R'$ with $P'(2, 3)$, $Q'(5, 3)$, $R'(2, 7)$.

**Method A — Using Rigid Motions:**
- Identify the transformation: Translation by $\langle 1, 2 \rangle$ (since $P(1, 1) \to P'(2, 3) = (1 + 1, 1 + 2)$)
- Verify it maps the entire triangle:
  - $Q(4, 1) \to Q'(5, 3)$ ✓
  - $R(1, 5) \to R'(2, 7)$ ✓
- Conclude: Since translation is a rigid motion, it preserves all distances and angles. Therefore, $\triangle PQR \cong \triangle P'Q'R'$.

**Method B — Using SSS Congruence:**
- Calculate side lengths of $\triangle PQR$:
  - $|PQ| = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (1-1)^2} = 3$
  - $|QR| = \sqrt{(1-4)^2 + (5-1)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$
  - $|RP| = \sqrt{(1-1)^2 + (1-5)^2} = 4$
- Calculate side lengths of $\triangle P'Q'R'$:
  - $|P'Q'| = \sqrt{(5-2)^2 + (3-3)^2} = 3$
  - $|Q'R'| = \sqrt{(2-5)^2 + (7-3)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$
  - $|R'P'| = \sqrt{(2-2)^2 + (3-7)^2} = 4$
- Conclude: All three pairs of sides are equal, so by SSS, $\triangle PQR \cong \triangle P'Q'R'$.

**Reflection:**
- **What becomes easier?** Rigid motions are faster if you can identify the transformation. SSS requires computing three distances.
- **What becomes harder?** Rigid motions require recognizing the transformation pattern. SSS is straightforward but computational.
- **When would you prefer each method?** Use rigid motions when the transformation is obvious (e.g., a simple translation). Use SSS when you need a formal proof or when the transformation is not obvious.

### Solution 4.4: Rotation Represented Multiple Ways
**Setup:** Rotate $(4, 0)$ by 90° counterclockwise about the origin.

**Method A — Coordinate Rule:**
$(x, y) \to (-y, x)$
$(4, 0) \to (0, 4)$

**Method B — Geometric Description:**
The point is at distance 4 from the origin (on the positive x-axis). After a 90° CCW rotation, it lies on the positive y-axis, still 4 units away. The rotated point is $(0, 4)$.

**Method C — Matrix Notation:**
The rotation matrix for 90° CCW is $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
$$R \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 4 + (-1) \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 4 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$$

All three methods give $(0, 4)$.

**Reflection:**
- **What becomes easier?** The coordinate rule is fastest for computation. The geometric description is easiest to visualize. The matrix notation is most general (works for any angle, any point).
- **What becomes harder?** The coordinate rule is hard to derive or remember if you forget the pattern. The geometric description is hard to generalize. The matrix notation requires matrix multiplication.
- **Which method is most efficient?** For a single point, the coordinate rule is most efficient. For many points or for proving properties, the matrix notation is most elegant.

### Solution 5.2: Composing Two Reflections
**Setup:** Reflect $(5, 2)$ over $x = 1$, then over $x = 4$.

**Step 1 — Reflect over $x = 1$:**
Distance from $(5, 2)$ to line $x = 1$ is $5 - 1 = 4$ units. Reflection is 4 units to the left of the line: $(1 - 4, 2) = (-3, 2)$.

**Step 2 — Reflect over $x = 4$:**
Distance from $(-3, 2)$ to line $x = 4$ is $4 - (-3) = 7$ units. Reflection is 7 units to the right of the line: $(4 + 7, 2) = (11, 2)$.

**Single Equivalent Transformation:**

### Solution 5.4: Dilation and Scale Factor
**Setup:** Rectangle $PQRS$ with $P(1, 1)$, $Q(5, 1)$, $R(5, 3)$, $S(1, 3)$ dilates to $P'Q'R'S'$ with $P'(0, 0)$, $Q'(8, 0)$, $R'(8, 4)$, $S'(0, 4)$.

**Finding the center of dilation:**
The center $C$ must satisfy: $P' = C + k(P - C)$ for some scale factor $k$.

Rearranging: $C = \frac{P' - kP}{1 - k}$ (for $k \ne 1$)

**From point $P$ and $P'$:**
$P(1, 1) \to P'(0, 0)$

If the center is at the origin (which we might guess), then $P'$ is $k$ times $P$:
$(0, 0) = k(1, 1)$, which gives $k = 0$. That doesn't make sense.

If the center is at some point $C(c_x, c_y)$:
$(0, 0) = C + k(P - C)$
$(0, 0) = (c_x, c_y) + k(1 - c_x, 1 - c_y)$
$(0, 0) = (c_x + k(1 - c_x), c_y + k(1 - c_y))$

This gives us:
$c_x + k(1 - c_x) = 0 \Rightarrow c_x(1 - k) = -k \Rightarrow c_x = \frac{k}{k - 1}$
$c_y + k(1 - c_y) = 0 \Rightarrow c_y(1 - k) = -k \Rightarrow c_y = \frac{k}{k - 1}$

So $C = (\frac{k}{k-1}, \frac{k}{k-1})$

**From point $Q$ and $Q'$:**
$Q(5, 1) \to Q'(8, 0)$

$(8, 0) = (c_x, c_y) + k(5 - c_x, 1 - c_y)$
$(8, 0) = (c_x + k(5 - c_x), c_y + k(1 - c_y))$

From the y-component:
$0 = c_y + k(1 - c_y)$, so $c_y = \frac{k}{k - 1}$ (same as before) ✓

From the x-component:
$8 = c_x + k(5 - c_x) = c_x(1 - k) + 5k = -\frac{k^2}{k-1} + 5k$

$8(k - 1) = -k^2 + 5k(k - 1)$
$8k - 8 = -k^2 + 5k^2 - 5k$
$8k - 8 = 4k^2 - 5k$
$0 = 4k^2 - 13k + 8$
$0 = (4k - 1)(k - 8)$... wait, let me recompute.

$4k^2 - 13k + 8 = 0$
Using the quadratic formula: $k = \frac{13 \pm \sqrt{169 - 128}}{8} = \frac{13 \pm \sqrt{41}}{8}$

Hmm, this is getting messy. Let me try a different approach: perhaps the center is at a nice point like $(-1, 4)$?

Check: If $C = (-1, 4)$ and scale factor $k$:
- $P(1, 1)$ to $P'(0, 0)$: $(0, 0) = (-1, 4) + k(1 - (-1), 1 - 4) = (-1, 4) + k(2, -3)$
  - x: $0 = -1 + 2k \Rightarrow k = 0.5$
  - y: $0 = 4 - 3k \Rightarrow k = \frac{4}{3}$

This doesn't match. Let me try a different center...

Actually, let me use a computational approach. For a dilation with center $C$ and scale factor $k$:
$$|P' - C| = k|P - C|$$

Let's denote $C = (c_x, c_y)$. Then:
$$|P'  - C| = k|P - C|$$
$$|(0, 0) - (c_x, c_y)| = k|(1, 1) - (c_x, c_y)|$$
$$\sqrt{c_x^2 + c_y^2} = k\sqrt{(1 - c_x)^2 + (1 - c_y)^2}$$

And similarly for other points. This is still complex.

**Simpler approach:** Assume the center is at the origin. Then scale factor $k = \frac{|P'|}{|P|}$.
$|P| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$
$|P'| = 0$

This doesn't work. Let me look at the rectangles:
- Original: width 4 (from $x = 1$ to $x = 5$), height 2 (from $y = 1$ to $y = 3$)
- Image: width 8 (from $x = 0$ to $x = 8$), height 4 (from $y = 0$ to $y = 4$)

Scale factor: $k = \frac{8}{4} = 2$ or $k = \frac{4}{2} = 2$. So $k = 2$.

**Finding center:**
$(0, 0) = C + 2(P - C) = C + 2(1, 1) - 2C = (2, 2) - C$
$C = (2, 2)$

**Verify with $Q$:**
$Q'$ should be $C + 2(Q - C) = (2, 2) + 2((5, 1) - (2, 2)) = (2, 2) + 2(3, -1) = (2, 2) + (6, -2) = (8, 0)$ ✓

**Center of dilation:** $(2, 2)$
**Scale factor:** $k = 2$

**I knew to use the property that images and pre-images are collinear with the center because I saw that a dilation was applied to a triangle (or rectangle).**

**If I saw a single point transformation, I might try different centers and solve algebraically for the scale factor.**

### Solution 5.7: Composition Order and Equivalence
**Setup:** Apply reflection over y-axis, then rotate 90° CCW. Compare to rotating 90° CCW, then reflecting over y-axis.

**Example point: $(1, 0)$**

**Order 1: Reflect over y-axis, then rotate 90° CCW**
- Reflect $(1, 0)$ over y-axis: $(1, 0) \to (-1, 0)$
- Rotate $(-1, 0)$ by 90° CCW: $(-1, 0) \to (0, -1)$
- **Final:** $(0, -1)$

**Order 2: Rotate 90° CCW, then reflect over y-axis**
- Rotate $(1, 0)$ by 90° CCW: $(1, 0) \to (0, 1)$
- Reflect $(0, 1)$ over y-axis: $(0, 1) \to (0, 1)$ (it's on the y-axis, so it's fixed!)
- **Final:** $(0, 1)$

$(0, -1) \ne (0, 1)$

**Conclusion:** The orders are NOT equivalent. Composition of transformations is generally not commutative.

**I knew to test a specific example because I saw that the order of two different types of transformations (reflection and rotation) was in question, and I know that order matters for different types.**

**If the problem asked about two translations, I would recognize immediately that order doesn't matter (translations commute).**
