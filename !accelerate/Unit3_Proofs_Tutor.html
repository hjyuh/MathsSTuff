Triangle Angle Sum Theorem

The three interior angles of any triangle add to 180°. The proof uses a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side.

∠B' ≅ ∠B and ∠C' ≅ ∠C as alternate interior angles. ∠B' + ∠A + ∠C' = 180° (straight line) ⇒ ∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°.

Exterior Angle Theorem

An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two remote (non-adjacent) interior angles.

Exterior ∠ACD = ∠A + ∠B (the two "remote" interior angles).

Proof of the Exterior Angle Theorem

#StatementReason
1∠A + ∠B + ∠ACB = 180°Triangle Angle Sum
2∠ACB + ∠ACD = 180°Linear pair
3∠A + ∠B + ∠ACB = ∠ACB + ∠ACDSubstitution (both = 180°)
4∠A + ∠B = ∠ACDSubtraction property

Triangle Inequality Theorem

For sides a, b, c: each side is shorter than the sum of the other two and longer than their difference: |b − c| < a < b + c.

Fast check: sort the sides and verify short + middle > long. That single check is enough.
  • 3, 4, 5 → 3+4 = 7 > 5 ✅
  • 2, 3, 6 → 2+3 = 5 < 6 ❌ (no triangle)
  • Range problem: if two sides are 5 and 8, the third must satisfy 3 < x < 13.

Isosceles Triangle Theorem (and converse)

If AB ≅ AC, then ∠B ≅ ∠C. And conversely: if ∠B ≅ ∠C, then AB ≅ AC.

In an isosceles triangle, the median from A to BC is also the altitude, the angle bisector, and the perpendicular bisector of BC — all the same line.

The 5 Triangle Congruence Theorems

Each one says: "if these 3 specific things match, all 6 parts match, so the triangles are congruent." The correspondence order of the vertices matters.

SSS — Side·Side·Side

Three pairs of sides congruent ⇒ triangles congruent.

Uniqueness argument: there is essentially one triangle with three given side lengths (ignore reflection). Rigid!

When to reach for it: you've been given or can derive all three side pairs as congruent.

SAS — Side·Angle between them·Side

Angle must be BETWEEN the two sides. The "included angle" — that's what makes SAS work.

Why it works: fix the angle, lay down the two sides from its vertex — the third side is forced.

Watch out: SAS ≠ SSA. If the angle isn't the included one, you can't use SAS.

ASA — Angle·Side between them·Angle

Side between the two known angles.

Two angles fix the third (angle-sum), so shape is determined. One side locks the size. Done.

AAS — Angle·Angle·Non-included Side

Side is NOT between the two known angles.

AAS = ASA in disguise (the third angle is determined by the other two, which slides the "known side" back between a pair of angles).

HL — Hypotenuse·Leg (right triangles only!)

Both have 90° angle + congruent hypotenuses + one congruent leg.

The Pythagorean theorem fills in the missing leg, so HL is essentially SSS in disguise — but only for right triangles.

Why SSA doesn't work — the ambiguous case

One angle, two sides (with the angle NOT between them): the arc from B can hit the top ray in two places, so the triangle isn't unique.

If you know ∠A, AB, and BC — with the known angle opposite one of the known sides — there can be 0, 1, or 2 valid triangles. So SSA is not a congruence theorem.

Exception: If the triangle is a right triangle, use HL instead. If you also know a second angle, switch to AAS.

CPCTC — Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent

CPCTC is what you use after you've proved two triangles congruent. It's the rule that lets you say "therefore those other sides/angles are equal too."

Ordering rule of thumb for every two-column proof in this unit:
  1. List the givens.
  2. Pick up extra free facts (reflexive side, vertical angles, definitions of midpoint/bisector, etc.).
  3. Cite one of SSS / SAS / ASA / AAS / HL to establish △ ≅ △.
  4. Use CPCTC to conclude whatever side or angle the problem asked about.

Worked Proof 2 — Vertical angles + SAS

Given: segments AC and BD intersect at point O, with AO ≅ CO and BO ≅ DO. Prove: △AOB ≅ △COD.

∠AOB and ∠COD are vertical angles — automatically congruent.
#StatementReason

Notice the two pairs of sides share the included angle at O — that's what makes SAS the right tool.

Worked Proof 3 — Parallel lines + ASA

Given: AB ∥ CD, and M is the midpoint of BC. Prove: △ABM ≅ △DCM.

M is the midpoint of BC. AB ∥ CD gives alternate interior angles, and vertical angles at M give the second angle.

Worked Proof 4 — CPCTC finishes the job

Given: AB ≅ AD and BC ≅ DC. Prove: ∠B ≅ ∠D.

AC is a shared ("reflexive") side. Kite-shape: AB ≅ AD, BC ≅ DC.

1AB ≅ ADGiven

The move: "same segment on both sides" = reflexive. Always check for it — it's free.

Practice — name the theorem

For each of the following, decide which congruence theorem (if any) applies. Toggle the answer.

1. In △ABC and △XYZ: AB ≅ XY, BC ≅ YZ, ∠B ≅ ∠Y.

SAS ∠B is the included angle between sides AB and BC.

2. ∠A ≅ ∠X, AB ≅ XY, ∠B ≅ ∠Y.

ASA AB is the included side between ∠A and ∠B.

3. ∠A ≅ ∠X, ∠B ≅ ∠Y, BC ≅ YZ.

AAS BC is a non-included side relative to ∠A, ∠X.

4. AB ≅ XY, BC ≅ YZ, ∠A ≅ ∠X.

NOT valid — SSA The angle is not between the two sides. Ambiguous case.

5. Right triangles with AC ≅ XZ (hypotenuses) and BC ≅ YZ (legs).

HL Right triangle + hypotenuse + leg.

6. AB ≅ XY, BC ≅ YZ, AC ≅ XZ.

SSS All three sides match.

7. Two sides 5 and 12. What's the range of the third side?

Triangle Inequality 7 < x < 17 (difference < x < sum).

8. In △ABC the exterior angle at C is 130° and ∠A = 70°. Find ∠B.

Exterior Angle Theorem 130° = ∠A + ∠B ⇒ ∠B = 60°.

Checklist before you start any proof

  1. Mark the diagram. Tick marks for equal sides; arcs for equal angles; a small square for a right angle.
  2. Find the "free" facts: reflexive shared side/angle, vertical angles, linear pair, midpoint splits, bisectors, parallel lines giving alt-int angles.
  3. Count what you have for the pair of triangles: three pieces? Which three? Then pick SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, or HL.
  4. Write the congruence statement in matching order (△ABC ≅ △XYZ means A↔X, B↔Y, C↔Z).
  5. If the "Prove" asks about something besides the triangle congruence, finish with CPCTC.
