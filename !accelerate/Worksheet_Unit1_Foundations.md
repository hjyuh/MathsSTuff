## MODULE 1: MODEL EXAMPLES — Complete Solutions

### Solution 1.1: Distance Between Two Points

**Problem:** Find the distance between $A = (2, 3)$ and $B = (5, 7)$.

**Why this matters:** The distance formula is your foundational tool for coordinate geometry. It comes from the Pythagorean theorem: if you draw a right triangle with the two points, the hypotenuse is the distance between them.

**The formula:**
$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$

**Step-by-step:**
$$d = \sqrt{(5 - 2)^2 + (7 - 3)^2}$$

Calculate the differences inside the parentheses:
- $x_2 - x_1 = 5 - 2 = 3$
- $y_2 - y_1 = 7 - 3 = 4$

Square each difference:
- $(3)^2 = 9$
- $(4)^2 = 16$

Add them:
$$d = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$$

**Answer:** The distance is **5 units**.

**Why this works:** Think of it as a 3-4-5 right triangle. The horizontal distance is 3, the vertical distance is 4, and the hypotenuse (the straight-line distance) is 5. This is your first "trigger"—when you see two points and need the straight-line distance between them, reach for the distance formula immediately.

**Pattern observed:** *The distance formula is always $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}$, and we square the changes in coordinates separately before adding them.*

---

### Solution 1.2: Midpoint of a Segment

**Problem:** Find the midpoint of the segment connecting $P = (-1, 4)$ and $Q = (3, -2)$.

**Why this matters:** The midpoint is the "average" of the two endpoints. This is useful for finding centers of figures, cutting segments in half, and identifying symmetry.

**The formula:**
$$M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$

**Step-by-step:**
$$M = \left(\frac{-1 + 3}{2}, \frac{4 + (-2)}{2}\right)$$

Calculate each coordinate:
- $x$-coordinate: $\frac{-1 + 3}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$
- $y$-coordinate: $\frac{4 - 2}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$

$$M = (1, 1)$$

**Answer:** The midpoint is **(1, 1)**.

**Why this works:** The midpoint splits the segment exactly in half. You can verify this by checking distances: $d(P, M) = \sqrt{(1-(-1))^2 + (1-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9} = \sqrt{13}$, and $d(Q, M) = \sqrt{(1-3)^2 + (1-(-2))^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9} = \sqrt{13}$. Both distances are equal, confirming that $M$ is the midpoint.

**The trigger:** When you see a segment and need to find the center or cut it in half, you're looking for the midpoint. Use the averaging formula.

**Pattern observed:** *The midpoint is simply the average of the $x$-coordinates and the average of the $y$-coordinates.*

---

### Solution 1.3: Calculate Slope

**Problem:** Find the slope of the line passing through $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 8)$.

**Why this matters:** Slope tells you how steep a line is and its direction. It's crucial for determining if lines are parallel (same slope) or perpendicular (opposite reciprocal slopes).

**The formula:**
$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

**Step-by-step:**
$$m = \frac{8 - 2}{4 - 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

**Answer:** The slope is **2** (or $m = 2$).

**What this means:** For every 1 unit you move to the right, you move 2 units up. The line is increasing steeply.

**The trigger:** When you have two points and need to know if lines are parallel, perpendicular, or just need the steepness, calculate slope using the rise-over-run formula.

**Pattern observed:** *Slope is always $\frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}$. Remember: *rise over run*.*

---

### Solution 1.4: Identify Angle Pair

**Problem:** Two lines intersect. One angle measures $45°$. What is the measure of the angle directly across from it (vertically opposite)?

**Why this matters:** Vertical angles (opposite angles formed when two lines intersect) are always equal. This is one of the most fundamental angle relationships.

**The relationship:** Vertical angles are congruent.

**Step-by-step:**
- You're given that one angle is $45°$.
- The angle directly across from it is a vertical angle.
- Vertical angles are always equal.
- Therefore, the opposite angle is also $45°$.

**Answer:** The angle is **45°**.

**Why this works:** When two lines cross, they form an "X" shape with four angles. The two angles at the top and bottom (opposite each other) are vertical angles and are equal. The two angles on the left and right are also vertical angles and are equal. But the top and left angles are supplementary (they add to $180°$).

**The trigger:** When you see angles formed by two intersecting lines and they're across from each other, those are vertical angles, and they're equal.

**Pattern observed:** *Vertical angles are always congruent (equal in measure).*

---

### Solution 1.5: Complementary Angles

**Problem:** Two angles are complementary. One angle measures $38°$. What is the measure of the other angle?

**Why this matters:** Complementary and supplementary angles are shortcuts to finding unknown angle measures.

**The definition:** Complementary angles add up to $90°$.

**Step-by-step:**
- You're given one angle: $38°$
- Let the unknown angle be $x$
- Since they're complementary: $38° + x = 90°$
- Solve: $x = 90° - 38° = 52°$

**Answer:** The other angle is **52°**.

**Verification:** $38° + 52° = 90°$ ✓

**The trigger:** When a problem says "complementary," immediately think "sum to 90°." When it says "supplementary," think "sum to 180°."

**Pattern observed:** *Complementary angles add to 90°. Supplementary angles add to 180°.*

---

### Solution 1.6: Supplementary Angles

**Problem:** Two angles are supplementary. One angle measures $112°$. What is the measure of the other angle?

**Why this matters:** Supplementary angles are everywhere—especially in linear pairs along a straight line or when a transversal cuts parallel lines.

**The definition:** Supplementary angles add up to $180°$.

**Step-by-step:**
- You're given one angle: $112°$
- Let the unknown angle be $x$
- Since they're supplementary: $112° + x = 180°$
- Solve: $x = 180° - 112° = 68°$

**Answer:** The other angle is **68°**.

**Verification:** $112° + 68° = 180°$ ✓

**Connection to straight lines:** If you have a straight line with a point on it, and two rays extending from that point, the angles they form on one side of the original line must be supplementary.

**The trigger:** When you see angles on a straight line or a linear pair, they're supplementary. They sum to $180°$.

**Pattern observed:** *Supplementary angles add to 180°. This happens whenever two angles form a straight line.*

---

### Solution 1.7: Parallel Lines and Slope

**Problem:** Line 1 has slope $m_1 = 2$. Line 2 has slope $m_2 = 2$. Are these lines parallel, perpendicular, or neither?

**Why this matters:** This is your first application of the slope criterion for parallel lines. Parallel lines have identical slopes (they never meet).

**The criterion:**
- **Parallel lines:** Same slope, $m_1 = m_2$
- **Perpendicular lines:** Slopes are negative reciprocals, $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$
- **Neither:** Any other relationship

**Step-by-step:**
- Line 1 has slope $2$
- Line 2 has slope $2$
- Since $m_1 = m_2$, the lines have the same slope
- Lines with the same slope are parallel

**Answer:** These lines are **parallel**.

**Why this works:** If two lines have the same slope, they're tilted at the same angle and will never intersect (unless they're the same line). That's the definition of parallel.

**The trigger:** When you have slopes of two lines and they're equal, the lines are parallel.

**Pattern observed:** *Lines are parallel if and only if they have the same slope.*

---

### Solution 1.8: Perpendicular Lines and Slope

**Problem:** Line 1 has slope $m_1 = 3$. Line 2 has slope $m_2 = -\frac{1}{3}$. Are these lines parallel, perpendicular, or neither?

**Why this matters:** Perpendicular lines have a special slope relationship—the slope of one is the negative reciprocal of the other.

**The key relationship:**
$$m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1 \quad \text{(perpendicular)}$$

**Step-by-step:**
- Line 1 has slope $m_1 = 3$
- Line 2 has slope $m_2 = -\frac{1}{3}$
- Check the product: $m_1 \cdot m_2 = 3 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{3}\right) = -1$
- Since the product is $-1$, the lines are perpendicular

**Answer:** These lines are **perpendicular**.

**Why this works:** The negative reciprocal relationship comes from the geometry of perpendicular lines. When two lines meet at a right angle, their slopes satisfy this special relationship. The 3 means "steep and positive" (rising to the right), while $-\frac{1}{3}$ means "gentle and negative" (falling to the right), and these directions are exactly perpendicular.

**The trigger:** When you're comparing slopes of two lines, check if they're equal (parallel) or if their product is $-1$ (perpendicular).

**Pattern observed:** *Lines are perpendicular if and only if the product of their slopes is $-1$, which means one slope is the negative reciprocal of the other.*

### Solution 1.9: Angle on a Straight Line

**Problem:** A straight line has a point on it. Two rays extend from that point, forming angles of $70°$ and $x°$ on one side of the line. These two angles form a linear pair. Find $x$.

**Why this matters:** A linear pair is two adjacent angles that form a straight line. They're always supplementary.

**The definition:** A linear pair is two adjacent angles whose non-common sides form a straight line. Linear pairs always sum to $180°$.

**Step-by-step:**
- You have a point on a line
- Two rays create angles of $70°$ and $x°$
- These form a linear pair
- Linear pairs sum to $180°$
- So: $70° + x = 180°$
- Solve: $x = 110°$

**Answer:** $x = $ **110°**.

**Verification:** $70° + 110° = 180°$ ✓

**Visual intuition:** Imagine a straight line. Now imagine a ray sticking out from a point on that line. It creates two angles on either side—one is $70°$, and the other is $x°$. Since the ray and the two sides of the line form a straight angle, the two angles must add to $180°$.

**The trigger:** When you see angles that form a linear pair (adjacent angles on a straight line), they're supplementary—sum to $180°$.

**Pattern observed:** *A linear pair is always supplementary because the two angles together form a straight line (180°).*

---

### Solution 1.10: Transversal and Corresponding Angles

**Problem:** Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. The transversal creates an angle of $65°$ at the first parallel line. What is the corresponding angle at the second parallel line?

**Why this matters:** Corresponding angles are one of the key angle relationships created when a transversal cuts parallel lines. They're always equal.

**The relationship:** When a transversal cuts two parallel lines, corresponding angles are congruent (equal).

**What are corresponding angles?** They're in the same position at each intersection point. If you think of the transversal as a "cutting line" going through the parallels, corresponding angles are in matching positions—both upper-left, both lower-right, etc.

**Step-by-step:**
- First parallel line has an angle of $65°$
- A transversal cuts through both parallel lines
- The corresponding angle at the second parallel line is in the same relative position
- Since the lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal
- Therefore, the corresponding angle is also $65°$

**Answer:** The corresponding angle is **65°**.

**Why this works:** Parallel lines have a special property: when cut by a transversal, they create consistent angle patterns. The corresponding angles are literally in the same position at each intersection, so they must be equal.

**The trigger:** When you see a transversal cutting two parallel lines and angles are in the same relative position, those are corresponding angles, and they're equal.

**Pattern observed:** *Corresponding angles are congruent when a transversal cuts parallel lines.*

### Solution 2.1: Parallel or Perpendicular?

**Problem:** Line $\ell_1$ passes through $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 4)$. Line $\ell_2$ passes through $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 3)$. Determine whether these lines are parallel, perpendicular, or neither.

**Why this problem is harder than Module 1:** You're now comparing two lines rather than just identifying properties of one. You need to calculate slope twice and then compare.

**Step-by-step:**

*Find the slope of $\ell_1$:*
$$m_1 = \frac{4 - 0}{2 - 0} = \frac{4}{2} = 2$$

*Find the slope of $\ell_2$:*
$$m_2 = \frac{3 - 1}{2 - 1} = \frac{2}{1} = 2$$

*Compare the slopes:*
- $m_1 = 2$ and $m_2 = 2$
- Since the slopes are equal, the lines are **parallel**

**Answer:** The lines are **parallel**.

**Check for perpendicularity (to be thorough):**
- If they were perpendicular, we'd need $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$
- But $2 \cdot 2 = 4 \neq -1$, so they're not perpendicular

**Trigger:** I used **slope calculation and comparison** because I saw **two lines with coordinates**, so I knew I needed to check if they had the same slope (parallel) or if their slopes multiplied to $-1$ (perpendicular). ### Solution 2.5: Finding a Fourth Vertex (Coordinate Geometry)

**Problem:** Three vertices of a parallelogram are $P = (0, 0)$, $Q = (4, 0)$, and $R = (5, 3)$. Find the fourth vertex $S$.

**Step-by-step:**

- If we assume the parallelogram is $PQRS$ (in order), then the diagonals are $\overline{PR}$ and $\overline{QS}$

*Use the fact that diagonals bisect each other:*
- The midpoint of $\overline{QS}$ must also be $M = \left(\frac{5}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$
- We know $Q = (4, 0)$ and we're looking for $S = (x, y)$
- The midpoint of $\overline{QS}$ is: $\left(\frac{4 + x}{2}, \frac{0 + y}{2}\right) = \left(\frac{5}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$

*Solve for $S$:*
$$\frac{4 + x}{2} = \frac{5}{2} \implies 4 + x = 5 \implies x = 1$$
$$\frac{0 + y}{2} = \frac{3}{2} \implies y = 3$$

So $S = (1, 3)$.

**Answer:** The fourth vertex is $S = (1, 3)$.

**Verification:**

- Midpoint of $\overline{QS}$: $\left(\frac{4+1}{2}, \frac{0+3}{2}\right) = \left(\frac{5}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\right)$ ✓

### Solution 2.6: Proving a Figure is a Rectangle Using Slope and Distance

**Problem:** Given four points: $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 0)$, $C = (4, 3)$, and $D = (0, 3)$. Prove that $ABCD$ is a rectangle.

**Why this is harder:** You need to combine multiple concepts—slope (to check right angles), distance (to check equal opposite sides), and careful organization of a proof.

**Properties of a rectangle:**
1. All four angles are right angles (or equivalently, opposite sides are parallel and adjacent sides are perpendicular)
2. Opposite sides are equal in length

**Step-by-step:**

*Check that all four angles are right angles:*

Slope of $\overline{AB}$:
$$m_{AB} = \frac{0 - 0}{4 - 0} = 0$$ (horizontal line)

Slope of $\overline{BC}$:
$$m_{BC} = \frac{3 - 0}{4 - 4} = \frac{3}{0} = \text{undefined}$$ (vertical line)

Since $m_{AB} = 0$ (horizontal) and $m_{BC}$ is undefined (vertical), and horizontal and vertical lines are perpendicular, angle $B$ is a right angle.

Slope of $\overline{CD}$:
$$m_{CD} = \frac{3 - 3}{0 - 4} = 0$$ (horizontal line)

Since $m_{BC}$ is undefined and $m_{CD} = 0$, angle $C$ is a right angle.

Slope of $\overline{DA}$:
$$m_{DA} = \frac{0 - 3}{0 - 0} = \text{undefined}$$ (vertical line)

Since $m_{CD} = 0$ and $m_{DA}$ is undefined, angle $D$ is a right angle.

Since angle $A$ is adjacent to both sides with slopes 0 and undefined, it's also a right angle.

*Check that opposite sides are equal:*

Distance $AB$:
$$d_{AB} = \sqrt{(4-0)^2 + (0-0)^2} = 4$$

Distance $CD$:
$$d_{CD} = \sqrt{(0-4)^2 + (3-3)^2} = 4$$

So $AB = CD$ ✓

Distance $BC$:
$$d_{BC} = \sqrt{(4-4)^2 + (3-0)^2} = 3$$

Distance $DA$:
$$d_{DA} = \sqrt{(0-0)^2 + (0-3)^2} = 3$$

So $BC = DA$ ✓

**Conclusion:**
- All four angles are right angles (proven via perpendicular slopes)
- Opposite sides are equal ($AB = CD = 4$ and $BC = DA = 3$)
- Therefore, $ABCD$ is a rectangle.

**Trigger:** I used **slope (to check perpendicularity) and distance formula (to check equal opposite sides)** because I saw **four coordinates and "prove it's a rectangle,"** which signaled that I needed to verify both the angle and side properties of rectangles. This will break when **the figure is not a rectangle**, in which case one of my checks would fail.

### Solution 3.1: The Perpendicular Slope Trap

**Problem:** Line $\ell$ has equation $y = 3x + 2$. Find the equation of the line perpendicular to $\ell$ passing through $(0, -5)$.

**The correct approach:**

*Identify the slope of $\ell$:*
$$m_\ell = 3$$

*Find the negative reciprocal:*
$$m_{\perp} = -\frac{1}{3}$$

*Use point-slope form:*
$$y - (-5) = -\frac{1}{3}(x - 0)$$
$$y + 5 = -\frac{1}{3}x$$
$$y = -\frac{1}{3}x - 5$$

**The trap:** A common mistake is to incorrectly find the "opposite" of the slope. Students might try:
- Using $-3$ instead of $-\frac{1}{3}$ (forgetting the reciprocal)
- Using $\frac{1}{3}$ instead of $-\frac{1}{3}$ (forgetting the negative)

**Why the trap exists:** "Opposite" is ambiguous. The correct operation is negative **reciprocal**, which involves two steps: negate AND flip the fraction.

### Solution 3.2: The Distance Formula Sign Trap

**Problem:** Find the distance between $(-2, 5)$ and $(3, -1)$.

**The incorrect approach (the trap):**
A student might try: "distance = $3 - (-2) + (-1) - 5 = 5 - 6 = -1$"

**What's wrong:**
1. **Sign error**: The student is subtracting the coordinates but not squaring them. Distances can never be negative.
2. **Conceptual error**: The student is adding the horizontal and vertical changes instead of using the Pythagorean theorem.
3. **Formula misapplication**: The distance formula requires squaring each difference, then taking the square root.

**The correct approach:**
$$d = \sqrt{(3 - (-2))^2 + ((-1) - 5)^2}$$
$$= \sqrt{5^2 + (-6)^2}$$
$$= \sqrt{25 + 36}$$
$$= \sqrt{61}$$

**Why the trap exists:** Students might think "distance is just the sum of changes," which would work if we didn't need to account for diagonal motion. But on a grid, movement is diagonal, and the Pythagorean theorem applies.

**Barrier Inventory:**

| Forgot the square root | Got a value that's too large | Technical (incomplete formula) | Always take the square root at the end |
| Didn't square the differences | Got a number that could be negative | Technical (order of operations) | Square each difference first, then add, then take the square root |

### Solution 3.5: The Co-Interior vs. Alternate Interior Trap

**Problem:** Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. A student identifies two angles on the same side of the transversal, both between the parallel lines (interior). The student calls these "alternate interior angles" and claims they're equal. Are they? Explain the error.

**Clarifying the angle types:**

**Alternate interior angles:** These are on **opposite sides** of the transversal and **between** the parallel lines. Alternate interior angles are congruent.

**Co-interior angles** (also called consecutive interior angles or same-side interior angles): These are on the **same side** of the transversal and **between** the parallel lines. Co-interior angles are supplementary (sum to $180°$).

**The error in the student's reasoning:**
The student confused the definitions. They said the angles were "on the same side of the transversal," which makes them co-interior, not alternate interior. Co-interior angles are supplementary, not equal.

**Example:**
Imagine two parallel horizontal lines cut by a transversal going from lower-left to upper-right. On the right side of the transversal:
- One angle is at the first (lower) parallel line, measuring $70°$
- The other angle is at the second (upper) parallel line, measuring $110°$
- These are co-interior angles, and $70° + 110° = 180°$ ✓

If these same angles were on opposite sides of the transversal, they'd be alternate interior angles and would both measure $70°$ (or both measure $110°$ if we're considering the different pair on the left side).

$$m_{AB} = \frac{6 - 2}{3 - 1} = \frac{4}{2} = 2$$

*Slope from $B$ to $C$:*
$$m_{BC} = \frac{10 - 6}{5 - 3} = \frac{4}{2} = 2$$

*Slope from $A$ to $C$:*
$$m_{AC} = \frac{10 - 2}{5 - 1} = \frac{8}{4} = 2$$

**Answer:** All slopes are equal ($m = 2$), so the three points are **collinear**.

---

**Part B — Distance Representation:**

**Solution:**

Three points are collinear if one of them lies on the segment between the other two. We can check this using the distance formula: if $d(A, B) + d(B, C) = d(A, C)$, then $B$ lies on segment $\overline{AC}$, and all three are collinear.

*Distance from $A$ to $B$:*
$$d(A, B) = \sqrt{(3-1)^2 + (6-2)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$$

*Distance from $B$ to $C$:*
$$d(B, C) = \sqrt{(5-3)^2 + (10-6)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$$

*Distance from $A$ to $C$:*
$$d(A, C) = \sqrt{(5-1)^2 + (10-2)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 64} = \sqrt{80} = 4\sqrt{5}$$

**Check:**
$$d(A, B) + d(B, C) = 2\sqrt{5} + 2\sqrt{5} = 4\sqrt{5} = d(A, C)$$ ✓

**Answer:** Since the sum of two distances equals the third, $B$ lies on segment $\overline{AC}$, so the three points are **collinear**.

---

**Reflection:**

- **Slope representation advantage:** Faster; uses the core idea (same slope means same line).
- **Slope representation disadvantage:** Requires computing slope three times (or at least twice); doesn't give intuition about the order of points.
- **Distance representation advantage:** Tells you not just that they're collinear, but also the order (which point is in the middle).
- **Distance representation disadvantage:** Slower; requires more calculation.
- **Which is faster?** Slope is faster.
- **Which gives more insight?** Distance gives more insight into whether one point is between the other two.
- **Which would you prefer?** On a homework problem, slope. On an exam, slope. If you need to know the order of points, distance.

### Solution 4.4: Perpendicularity Check

**Part A — Slope Representation:**

**Problem:** Are the line segments from $A = (0, 0)$ to $B = (3, 4)$ and from $C = (3, 4)$ to $D = (7, 1)$ perpendicular?

**Solution:**

*Slope of $\overline{AB}$:*
$$m_{AB} = \frac{4 - 0}{3 - 0} = \frac{4}{3}$$

*Slope of $\overline{CD}$:*
$$m_{CD} = \frac{1 - 4}{7 - 3} = \frac{-3}{4}$$

*Check the product:*
$$m_{AB} \cdot m_{CD} = \frac{4}{3} \cdot \left(\frac{-3}{4}\right) = -1$$

**Answer:** Since the product of the slopes is $-1$, the segments are **perpendicular**.

---

**Part B — Dot Product Representation:**

**Solution:**

Using vectors, we compute the dot product of the direction vectors.

*Direction vector of $\overline{AB}$:*
$$\vec{AB} = (3 - 0, 4 - 0) = (3, 4)$$

*Direction vector of $\overline{CD}$:*
$$\vec{CD} = (7 - 3, 1 - 4) = (4, -3)$$

*Dot product:*
$$\vec{AB} \cdot \vec{CD} = (3)(4) + (4)(-3) = 12 - 12 = 0$$

**Answer:** Since the dot product is 0, the vectors are **perpendicular**, so the segments are **perpendicular**.

---

**Reflection:**

- **Slope method:** Check if $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$. Intuitive; directly uses the "opposite reciprocal" criterion.
- **Dot product method:** Check if the dot product is 0. More general (works in any dimension); reveals that perpendicularity is about orthogonality of vectors.
- **Why is the dot product useful?** It generalizes to 3D and higher dimensions, where slopes don't make sense. It also connects to linear algebra and physics.
- **Relationship:** Both methods are equivalent. The slope method is a 2D specialization of the dot product method.

### Solution 5.6: Points on a Line

Check if $(4, 10)$ satisfies this:

$$10 \neq 9$$

The point does **not** lie on the line.

### Solution 6.2: Multi-Step Coordinate Proof

**Problem:** Three vertices of a rectangle are $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (8, 0)$, and $C = (8, 6)$. Find the fourth vertex $D$, then prove that $ABCD$ is indeed a rectangle by showing:
1. All four sides have the correct lengths
2. The diagonals are equal in length

---

**Before solving — Candidate approaches:**

1. **Parallelogram property:** Use the fact that in a parallelogram (which includes rectangles), the diagonals bisect each other. Set up equations using midpoints.
2. **Vector addition:** Recognize that in a rectangle, $\vec{AB} + \vec{BC} = \vec{AC}$, and use vectors to find $D$.
3. **Geometric intuition:** Observe that $A$, $B$, $C$ form three vertices of a rectangle with $B$ and $C$ having the same $x$-coordinate (so $BC$ is vertical). Thus $D$ must be directly above $A$ at height 6.

---

**Prediction:** The geometric intuition (approach 3) is fastest. Since $BC$ is vertical (same $x$-coordinate), and we're in a rectangle, $AD$ must also be vertical. So $D$ has the same $x$-coordinate as $A$ and the same $y$-coordinate as $C$.

---

**Solution:**

*Find the fourth vertex $D$:*

We have:
- $A = (0, 0)$
- $B = (8, 0)$
- $C = (8, 6)$

Observation: $B$ and $C$ have the same $x$-coordinate (both at $x = 8$), so $\overline{BC}$ is vertical with length 6.

In a rectangle, opposite sides are parallel and equal. So $\overline{AD}$ must also be vertical with length 6, starting from $A = (0, 0)$. Thus $D = (0, 6)$.

---

*Prove all four sides have the correct lengths:*

$$d(A, B) = \sqrt{(8-0)^2 + (0-0)^2} = 8$$
$$d(B, C) = \sqrt{(8-8)^2 + (6-0)^2} = 6$$
$$d(C, D) = \sqrt{(0-8)^2 + (6-6)^2} = 8$$
$$d(D, A) = \sqrt{(0-0)^2 + (0-6)^2} = 6$$

Opposite sides: $d(A,B) = d(C,D) = 8$ ✓ and $d(B,C) = d(D,A) = 6$ ✓

---

*Prove the diagonals are equal in length:*

$$d(A, C) = \sqrt{(8-0)^2 + (6-0)^2} = \sqrt{64 + 36} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$
$$d(B, D) = \sqrt{(0-8)^2 + (6-0)^2} = \sqrt{64 + 36} = \sqrt{100} = 10$$

The diagonals are equal: $d(A,C) = d(B,D) = 10$ ✓

**Conclusion:** Since opposite sides are equal and the diagonals are equal, $ABCD$ is a rectangle.

### Solution 6.3: Investigating a Contradiction in Angle Measures

**Problem:** Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. At the first intersection point, an angle measures $(5x - 15)°$. At the second intersection point, the corresponding angle measures $(2x + 45)°$.

Wait—this doesn't make sense. If the lines are parallel, corresponding angles should be equal. So what went wrong in this problem?

Investigate this situation:
- Set the angles equal and solve for $x$
- Find the angle measures
- Determine which property of parallel lines was actually used (maybe they're not corresponding angles after all)
- Propose what type of angles these actually are
- Write a clear explanation of your findings

---

**Before solving — Candidate approaches to investigate:**

1. **Assume they're corresponding angles (as stated):** Set them equal, find the contradiction, and propose a revision.
2. **Test alternate interior angles:** These are also equal for parallel lines. Set them equal and see if it makes sense.
3. **Test co-interior angles:** These are supplementary for parallel lines. Set their sum to $180°$ and see if it makes sense.

---

**Prediction:** If we set the angles equal (assuming they're corresponding), we'll get a valid value for $x$. But this reveals that the problem statement had a typo or the angles are mislabeled. Then, testing co-interior angles will likely make sense.

---

**Investigation:**

*Set the angles equal (assuming they're corresponding):*
$$(5x - 15) = (2x + 45)$$
$$3x = 60$$
$$x = 20$$

*Find the angle measures:*
- First angle: $(5(20) - 15)° = (100 - 15)° = 85°$
- Second angle: $(2(20) + 45)° = (40 + 45)° = 85°$

**So the angles are equal!** If they were actually corresponding angles, they should be equal for parallel lines. This is consistent.

But wait—let me reconsider the problem. Maybe the angles are **not** corresponding; maybe they're **co-interior** angles, but I misread the setup.

*Test co-interior angles:*

If these are co-interior angles (also called consecutive interior or same-side interior), they should be **supplementary** (sum to $180°$), not equal:
$$(5x - 15) + (2x + 45) = 180$$
$$7x + 30 = 180$$
$$7x = 150$$
$$x = \frac{150}{7} \approx 21.43°$$

This gives a different (fractional) value.

---

**Revealing the issue:**

The problem as originally stated is **not actually contradictory** if the angles are corresponding angles. The confusion likely arises from the problem statement itself, which says "this doesn't make sense" but then provides angles that perfectly satisfy the corresponding angles property.

**What actually went wrong (the "trap"):**

The problem tries to trick you into thinking there's an error, but there isn't one—IF the angles are truly corresponding. However, if the problem intended for the angles to be described differently (e.g., "co-interior" instead of "corresponding"), then we'd expect them to be supplementary, not equal.

---

**Clear explanation of the findings:**

Given the setup and the algebra, the two angles measure $85°$ each, which is exactly what we'd expect for corresponding angles on parallel lines cut by a transversal. The angles are **corresponding angles**, and they are equal, which is the correct property.

However, if the problem statement had said "co-interior angles" instead, the two expressions would need to sum to $180°$, giving $x = \frac{150}{7}$ and angles of approximately $114.3°$ and $65.7°$.

**Conclusion:**

There was no actual contradiction in this problem—the setup is consistent with the corresponding angles property. The "trick" was that the problem statement suggested there was an error when there wasn't one.

- **Why is it important to double-check that a problem makes sense?** Sometimes problem statements contain errors or are designed to trick you into second-guessing yourself. Working through the algebra first, then interpreting the result, helps you catch such tricks.
