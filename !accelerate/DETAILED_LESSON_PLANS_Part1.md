### [0:05 - 0:12] BLOCK 1 — Points, Lines, Angles (The Language)

**SAY:** "Let's start from absolute zero. Here's the foundation."

**DRAW on screen:**
- A single point. Label it "A"
- A line passing through it. Mark arrows on both ends.
- A line segment with two endpoints. Mark the endpoints clearly.
- A ray starting from one point, going in one direction, with one arrow.

**SAY:**
- "A POINT is just a location. No size. We label it with a capital letter: A."
- "A LINE goes forever in both directions. We write it as AB with a double arrow: ↔AB. It has no endpoints."
- "A SEGMENT is a piece of a line WITH two endpoints. We write it as AB̅ (AB with a bar). It has a measurable length."
- "A RAY starts at one point and goes forever in one direction. We write it as →AB — the first letter is always the endpoint."

**SAY:** "Now angles. An angle is formed by two rays sharing an endpoint (the vertex)."

**DRAW:** An angle. Label the vertex V and the two rays.

**SAY:** "There are four angle types you need to know cold:"

**WRITE on screen:**
- "ACUTE: less than 90° — sharp, pointy"
- "RIGHT: exactly 90° — marked with a little square"
- "OBTUSE: between 90° and 180° — wide, fat"
- "STRAIGHT: exactly 180° — literally a straight line"

**SAY:** "Now the angle PAIRS — these show up constantly:"

**WRITE and EXPLAIN:**
- "COMPLEMENTARY: two angles that ADD to 90°. Think: Co = Corner (90°). Example: 30° and 60°."
- "SUPPLEMENTARY: two angles that ADD to 180°. Think: Su = Straight line (180°). Example: 110° and 70°."
- "VERTICAL ANGLES: when two lines cross, the angles ACROSS from each other are EQUAL. Always equal."
- "LINEAR PAIR: two angles that are supplementary AND adjacent (sitting next to each other on a line)."

**CHECK PROBLEM 1:**
"Two angles are vertical. One is 73°. What's the other?"

**Wait for answer.** Expected: 73°

**SAY:** "Right. Vertical angles are ALWAYS equal. That's the whole thing."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 2:**
"Two angles are supplementary. One is 115°. What's the other?"

**Wait for answer.** Expected: 65° (because 180 − 115 = 65)

**SAY:** "Supplementary means they add to 180°. So if one is 115°, the other has to be 65°."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 3:**
"Angle A and Angle B are complementary. Angle A is (2x + 10)° and Angle B is (3x)°. Find x."

**Walk them through if stuck:**
- "They're complementary, so they add to 90°. Set up the equation."
- "(2x + 10) + 3x = 90"
- "5x + 10 = 90"
- "5x = 80"
- "x = 16"
- "So Angle A = 2(16) + 10 = 42°, Angle B = 3(16) = 48°. Check: 42 + 48 = 90 ✓"

**IF CONFUSED:** "Complementary = Corner (both start with C, and a corner is 90°). Supplementary = Straight (both start with S, and a straight line is 180°). Use that mnemonic."

---

### [0:12 - 0:22] BLOCK 2 — Parallel Lines & Transversals

**SAY:** "This is one of those things that looks complicated with all the angle names, but there's actually a super simple pattern once you see it."

**DRAW on screen:**
```
Two horizontal parallel lines. Label them ℓ₁ and ℓ₂.
Draw a diagonal line crossing both (the transversal).
Label the 8 angles:

         1 | 2
    ——————+—————— ℓ₁
         3 | 4

         5 | 6
    ——————+—————— ℓ₂
         7 | 8
```

**SAY:** "When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, it creates 8 angles. Here's the pattern — and there's really only ONE rule:"

**POINT and SAY:**
- "CORRESPONDING angles are in the same position at each intersection: ∠1 & ∠5, ∠2 & ∠6, ∠3 & ∠7, ∠4 & ∠8. They're EQUAL."
- "ALTERNATE INTERIOR angles are between the parallel lines, on opposite sides: ∠3 & ∠6, ∠4 & ∠5. They're EQUAL."
- "ALTERNATE EXTERIOR angles are outside the parallel lines, on opposite sides: ∠1 & ∠8, ∠2 & ∠7. They're EQUAL."
- "CO-INTERIOR angles (same-side interior) are between the parallel lines, on the SAME side: ∠3 & ∠5, ∠4 & ∠6. They ADD to 180°."

**SAY:** "Here's the cheat code: if two angles LOOK like they're in matching spots, they're equal. If they're on the same side between the lines, they add to 180°. That's it."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 4:**
"In my diagram, ∠1 = 65°. Find every other angle."

**Walk through:**
- ∠1 = 65° (given)
- ∠2 = 115° (linear pair with ∠1: 180 − 65)
- ∠3 = 115° (vertical with ∠2)
- ∠4 = 65° (vertical with ∠1)
- ∠5 = 65° (corresponding with ∠1)
- ∠6 = 115° (corresponding with ∠2)
- ∠7 = 115° (corresponding with ∠3)
- ∠8 = 65° (corresponding with ∠4)

**SAY:** "See? Once you know ONE angle, you know ALL EIGHT. That's the power of parallel lines."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 5:**
"∠3 = (4x + 12)° and ∠6 = (6x − 8)°. These are alternate interior angles. Find x."

**Guide:**
- "Alternate interior angles are equal."
- "4x + 12 = 6x − 8"
- "20 = 2x"
- "x = 10"
- "Check: ∠3 = 4(10) + 12 = 52°, ∠6 = 6(10) − 8 = 52° ✓"

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 6:**
"∠4 = (3x + 15)° and ∠6 = (5x + 5)°. These are co-interior (same-side interior) angles. Find x."

**Guide:**
- "Co-interior angles ADD to 180°."
- "(3x + 15) + (5x + 5) = 180"
- "8x + 20 = 180"
- "8x = 160"
- "x = 20"
- "Check: ∠4 = 3(20) + 15 = 75°, ∠6 = 5(20) + 5 = 105°. Sum: 75 + 105 = 180 ✓"

**IF CONFUSED:** "Ask yourself two questions: (1) Are they between the parallel lines or outside? (2) Are they on the same side or opposite sides of the transversal? Answer those and you know the relationship."

### [0:22 - 0:37] BLOCK 3 — Coordinate Geometry (Distance, Midpoint, Slope, Lines)

**SAY:** "Now we bring in coordinates. Three formulas and one skill. Get these down and you're set for coordinate geometry for the rest of the year."

---

#### DISTANCE FORMULA

**DRAW on coordinate plane:**
- Plot points A(1, 2) and B(4, 6)
- Draw a right triangle: horizontal leg from (1,2) to (4,2), vertical leg from (4,2) to (4,6), hypotenuse from (1,2) to (4,6)

**SAY:** "The distance formula is literally just the Pythagorean theorem on a coordinate plane. Watch:"
- "Horizontal distance: 4 − 1 = 3"
- "Vertical distance: 6 − 2 = 4"
- "By Pythagorean theorem: d = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5"

**WRITE the formula on screen:**
```
d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
```

**SAY:** "That's all it is. Subtract the x's, subtract the y's, square both, add, take the square root."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 7:**
"Find the distance between (−2, 3) and (4, −1)."

**Walk through:**
- d = √[(4 − (−2))² + (−1 − 3)²]
- d = √[(6)² + (−4)²]
- d = √[36 + 16]
- d = √52
- d = √(4 × 13) = 2√13

**SAY:** "Don't panic about simplifying radicals. √52 — you pull out perfect squares. 52 = 4 × 13, so √52 = 2√13."

---

#### MIDPOINT FORMULA

**SAY:** "Midpoint is even simpler. You just AVERAGE the x's and AVERAGE the y's."

**WRITE the formula:**
```
M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
```

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 8:**
"Find the midpoint of (−2, 3) and (4, −1)."

**Walk through:**
- M = ((−2 + 4)/2, (3 + (−1))/2)
- M = (2/2, 2/2)
- M = (1, 1)

**SAY:** "That's it. Average the x's, average the y's. Done."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 9 (Reverse midpoint — this is common):**
"The midpoint of A(2, 5) and B(x, y) is M(6, 3). Find B."

**Walk through:**
- For x: (2 + x)/2 = 6 → 2 + x = 12 → x = 10
- For y: (5 + y)/2 = 3 → 5 + y = 6 → y = 1
- So B = (10, 1)

**SAY:** "Sometimes they flip it and give you the midpoint and one endpoint, then ask for the other. Just set up the averages and solve."

---

#### SLOPE

**WRITE the formula:**
```
m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) = rise/run
```

**SAY:** "Slope tells you how steep a line is. Positive slope goes up-right. Negative slope goes down-right. Zero slope is flat (horizontal). Undefined slope is vertical."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 10:**
"Find the slope between (1, 3) and (4, 9)."

**Walk through:**
- m = (9 − 3)/(4 − 1) = 6/3 = 2

**SAY:** "Now here's the CRITICAL rule for parallel and perpendicular lines:"

**WRITE on screen:**
- "PARALLEL lines have the SAME slope. If line A has slope 2, line B parallel to it also has slope 2."
- "PERPENDICULAR lines have NEGATIVE RECIPROCAL slopes. If line A has slope 2, the perpendicular line has slope −1/2. You flip the fraction AND change the sign."

**WRITE the rules:**
```
Parallel: m₁ = m₂
Perpendicular: m₁ × m₂ = −1
```

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 11:**
"Line A has slope 3/5. What slope is parallel to it? Perpendicular?"

**Answer:**
- Parallel: 3/5
- Perpendicular: −5/3 (flip and negate)

**SAY:** "To get the perpendicular slope: take 3/5, flip it to 5/3, then negate to −5/3."

---

#### WRITING EQUATIONS OF LINES

**SAY:** "Point-slope form is the most useful one you'll see. Here it is:"

**WRITE:**
```
y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)
```

**SAY:** "You need a point (x₁, y₁) and a slope m. That's it. Plug in and simplify."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 12:**
"Write the equation of the line through (3, −2) with slope 4."

**Walk through:**
- y − (−2) = 4(x − 3)
- y + 2 = 4x − 12
- y = 4x − 14 (slope-intercept form)

**SAY:** "If they want slope-intercept, just solve for y. If they're okay with point-slope, leave it as y + 2 = 4(x − 3)."

---

**CHECK PROBLEM 13 (The big one — this is a classic exam problem):**
"Write the equation of the line through (2, 5) perpendicular to y = 3x + 1."

**Guide them:**
- "First, what's the original slope?" → 3
- "What's the perpendicular slope?" → −1/3
- "Now use point-slope with the point (2, 5) and slope −1/3."

**Walk through:**
- y − 5 = −1/3(x − 2)
- y − 5 = −1/3 x + 2/3
- y = −1/3 x + 2/3 + 5
- y = −1/3 x + 17/3 (slope-intercept)

**SAY:** "This type of problem — 'write the equation perpendicular/parallel to a given line through a given point' — is on literally every geometry exam. The steps are always the same: (1) find the original slope, (2) get the new slope (same for parallel, negative reciprocal for perpendicular), (3) plug into point-slope with the given point."

**IF STRUGGLING WITH PERPENDICULAR SLOPES:** "Think of it as two operations in order: flip the fraction, then change the sign. Slope 2 = 2/1 → flip to 1/2 → negate to −1/2."

---

**LIVE PROBLEM A:**
"Points A(−3, 1), B(5, 1), C(5, 7). Find the perimeter of triangle ABC."

**LIVE PROBLEM B:**
"Line ℓ passes through (1, 4) and (3, 10). Line m passes through (2, 1) and (4, 7). Are ℓ and m parallel, perpendicular, or neither?"

**Walk through:**
- Slope of ℓ: (10 − 4)/(3 − 1) = 6/2 = 3
- Slope of m: (7 − 1)/(4 − 2) = 6/2 = 3
- Same slopes → PARALLEL

**LIVE PROBLEM C:**
"Given A(1, 2) and B(7, 6). Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of AB̅."

**SAY:** "This one combines midpoint, slope, perpendicular slope, AND writing equations. It's a greatest hits problem."

**Walk through:**
- Step 1: Midpoint of AB = ((1+7)/2, (2+6)/2) = (4, 4)
- Step 2: Slope of AB = (6−2)/(7−1) = 4/6 = 2/3
- Step 3: Perpendicular slope = −3/2
- Step 4: Using point (4, 4) and slope −3/2:
  - y − 4 = −3/2(x − 4)
  - y = −3/2 x + 6 + 4
  - y = −3/2 x + 10

**SAY:** "Notice we found the perpendicular slope, then used the MIDPOINT as our point (because the perpendicular bisector passes through the midpoint by definition)."

### [0:47 - 0:53] COMMON TRAPS

**SAY:** "Let me show you the mistakes that literally everyone makes at first."

---

**TRAP 1 — Perpendicular slope:**
"The perpendicular slope of 2 is NOT 1/2. It's −1/2. You MUST negate. People forget the negative all the time. It kills exam scores."

---

**TRAP 2 — Distance formula signs:**
"In the distance formula, everything gets squared, so negatives disappear. Don't stress about subtraction order. (4−1)² and (1−4)² both give 9. The squaring removes the sign issue."

---

**TRAP 3 — Co-interior angles vs. Alternate interior angles:**
"Alternate interior angles are EQUAL. Co-interior (same-side interior) angles ADD to 180°. People mix these up because they're both 'interior.' The key: alternate means opposite sides of the transversal → equal. Same-side → add to 180°."

---

**TRAP 4 — Vertical angles aren't adjacent:**
"Vertical angles are ACROSS from each other when lines cross (like an X shape). They're not the ones sitting next to each other — those are linear pairs, and they're supplementary."

### [0:05 - 0:10] BLOCK 1 — What's a Transformation?

**SAY:** "A transformation is a function that takes a point and moves it to a new location. The original is called the PRE-IMAGE. The result is called the IMAGE. That's the only vocab you need."

"There are four types: translations (slide), reflections (flip), rotations (spin), and dilations (resize)."

"The first three — translations, reflections, rotations — are called RIGID MOTIONS or ISOMETRIES. They preserve everything: distances, angles, area. The shape doesn't change at all."

"Dilations change the size. So they're NOT rigid motions."

"Why does this matter? Because in geometry, CONGRUENT means you can get from one figure to the other using ONLY rigid motions. If you need a dilation, they're SIMILAR, not congruent. That distinction is huge."

---

### [0:10 - 0:25] BLOCK 2 — The Coordinate Rules

**SAY:** "This block is the core. I'm going to give you every coordinate rule for transformations. Learn these. You'll apply them constantly."

---

#### TRANSLATIONS

**SAY:** "Translation = slide. Every point moves the same direction and distance."

**WRITE the rule:**
```
(x, y) → (x + a, y + b)
where <a, b> is the translation vector
```

**SAY:** "Positive a = move right. Negative a = move left. Positive b = move up. Negative b = move down."

**EXAMPLE:** "If I say 'translate by vector <−3, 2>', that means slide LEFT 3 and UP 2."

---

**PROBLEM 14:**
"Translate triangle A(1, 3), B(4, 3), C(4, 7) by vector <−3, 2>."

**Walk through:**
- A(1, 3) → A'(1 − 3, 3 + 2) = A'(−2, 5)
- B(4, 3) → B'(4 − 3, 3 + 2) = B'(1, 5)
- C(4, 7) → C'(4 − 3, 7 + 2) = C'(1, 9)

**SAY:** "See how every point moved the same way? That's translation."

---

#### REFLECTIONS

**SAY:** "Reflection = flip over a line. Here are the 4 you MUST memorize:"

**WRITE on screen:**
```
Over x-axis:    (x, y) → (x, −y)       [y changes sign]
Over y-axis:    (x, y) → (−x, y)      [x changes sign]
Over y = x:     (x, y) → (y, x)       [swap x and y]
Over y = −x:    (x, y) → (−y, −x)     [swap AND negate both]
```

**SAY:** "The first two are easy — just negate the coordinate that the axis is NOT. Reflecting over the x-axis? The x stays still, the y flips sign."

---

**PROBLEM 15:**
"Reflect point (3, −5) over the x-axis."

**Answer:** (3, 5)

**SAY:** "x stays, y flips: (3, −5) → (3, 5)."

---

**PROBLEM 16:**
"Reflect point (3, −5) over y = x."

**Answer:** (−5, 3)

**SAY:** "Swap: (3, −5) → (−5, 3). The line y = x is where x = y, so reflecting swaps their values."

---

**PROBLEM 17:**
"Reflect triangle A(1, 2), B(4, 2), C(2, 5) over the y-axis."

**Walk through:**
- A(1, 2) → A'(−1, 2)
- B(4, 2) → B'(−4, 2)
- C(2, 5) → C'(−2, 5)

**SAY:** "x changes sign, y stays. That's reflecting over the y-axis."

---

**IF CONFUSED ABOUT y=x:** "Think of it this way — the line y = x is the diagonal where x-coordinates equal y-coordinates. Reflecting over it swaps their roles. A point at (3, 5) becomes (5, 3)."

---

#### ROTATIONS

**SAY:** "Rotations around the origin. Three rules to know:"

**WRITE:**
```
90° CCW:        (x, y) → (−y, x)
180°:           (x, y) → (−x, −y)
270° CCW:       (x, y) → (y, −x)
```

**SAY:** "The mnemonic for 90° CCW: negate the y, then swap. For 180°: negate both. For 270° CCW: it's the reverse of 90°."

---

**PROBLEM 18:**
"Rotate (4, −2) by 90° counterclockwise about the origin."

**Walk through:**
- Rule: (x, y) → (−y, x)
- (4, −2) → (−(−2), 4) = (2, 4)

---

**PROBLEM 19:**
"Rotate (4, −2) by 180° about the origin."

**Walk through:**
- Rule: (x, y) → (−x, −y)
- (4, −2) → (−4, 2)

---

**PROBLEM 20:**
"Rotate (4, −2) by 270° CCW about the origin."

**Walk through:**
- Rule: (x, y) → (y, −x)
- (4, −2) → (−2, −4)

---

**CHECK:** "Quick: 90° CW is the same as what in CCW?"

**Expected:** 270° CCW

**SAY:** "Right. Clockwise and counterclockwise go in opposite directions, so 90° one way = 270° the other way."

---

#### DILATIONS

**SAY:** "Dilation = resize from a center point. If the center is the origin:"

**WRITE:**
```
(x, y) → (kx, ky)
where k is the scale factor
```

**SAY:**
- "k > 1: enlargement (bigger)"
- "0 < k < 1: reduction (smaller)"
- "k < 0: enlargement/reduction + rotation 180°"

---

**PROBLEM 21:**
"Dilate triangle A(2, 4), B(6, 2), C(4, 8) by scale factor 1/2 from the origin."

**Walk through:**
- A(2, 4) → A'(1 × 2, 1/2 × 4) = A'(1, 2)
- B(6, 2) → B'(1/2 × 6, 1/2 × 2) = B'(3, 1)
- C(4, 8) → C'(1/2 × 4, 1/2 × 8) = C'(2, 4)

**SAY:** "Remember: dilation is NOT a rigid motion. It changes lengths. The image is SIMILAR to the pre-image, not congruent. That's a crucial difference."

---

### [0:25 - 0:35] BLOCK 3 — Compositions, Symmetry, Congruence

**SAY:** "A composition is doing two (or more) transformations in sequence. And here's the thing: THE ORDER MATTERS."

---

**PROBLEM 22:**
"Take point P(2, 3). First reflect over the x-axis, then translate by <4, 1>."

**Walk through:**
- Reflect over x-axis: (2, 3) → (2, −3)
- Translate by <4, 1>: (2, −3) → (2 + 4, −3 + 1) = (6, −2)
- Final answer: P'' = (6, −2)

---

**PROBLEM 23 (Same point, same transformations, different order):**
"Take P(2, 3). First translate by <4, 1>, then reflect over the x-axis."

**Walk through:**
- Translate by <4, 1>: (2, 3) → (6, 4)
- Reflect over x-axis: (6, 4) → (6, −4)
- Final answer: P'' = (6, −4)

**SAY:** "See? (6, −2) ≠ (6, −4). ORDER MATTERS. Always do transformations in the exact order given. Left to right."

---

### Symmetry (Quick)

**SAY:** "Two types:"

**WRITE:**
- "LINE SYMMETRY: fold the figure along a line and both halves match perfectly. The fold line is the line of symmetry."
- "ROTATIONAL SYMMETRY: rotate the figure less than 360° and it looks identical. The ORDER is how many times it matches in a full rotation."

---

**PROBLEM 24:**
"How many lines of symmetry does a regular hexagon have?"

**Answer:** 6

"What's its order of rotational symmetry?"

**Answer:** 6

**SAY:** "A regular hexagon has 6 lines of symmetry (three through opposite vertices, three through opposite edge midpoints) and rotational symmetry of order 6 (it looks the same every 60°)."

---

### Congruence via Transformations

**SAY:** "Two figures are CONGRUENT if and only if one can be mapped onto the other using rigid motions — translation, reflection, rotation. NOT dilation. If you need a dilation, they're SIMILAR, not congruent."

### [0:35 - 0:47] LIVE PROBLEMS

**LIVE PROBLEM D:**
"Triangle ABC has vertices A(0, 0), B(3, 0), C(0, 4). Apply: reflect over y-axis, then rotate 90° CCW about the origin."

**Walk through:**
- Reflect over y-axis:
  - A(0, 0) → A'(0, 0)
  - B(3, 0) → B'(−3, 0)
  - C(0, 4) → C'(0, 4)
- Rotate 90° CCW: (x, y) → (−y, x)
  - A'(0, 0) → A''(0, 0)
  - B'(−3, 0) → B''(0, −3)
  - C'(0, 4) → C''(−4, 0)

**SAY:** "Notice A doesn't move at all (it's at the origin). That's important to track."

---

**LIVE PROBLEM E:**
"Point P was transformed to P'(−3, 7). The transformation was a reflection over the x-axis. What was the original point P?"

**Walk through:**
- Reflection over x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y)
- We know (x, −y) = (−3, 7)
- So x = −3 and −y = 7, which means y = −7
- Original point: P = (−3, −7)

**SAY:** "This is working BACKWARDS. You're given the image and need to find the pre-image."

---

**LIVE PROBLEM F:**
"Describe a sequence of rigid motions that maps triangle (1, 1), (4, 1), (1, 3) to triangle (−1, −1), (−4, −1), (−1, −3)."

**Walk through:**
- Compare the points: (1, 1) → (−1, −1), (4, 1) → (−4, −1), (1, 3) → (−1, −3)
- Each coordinate is negated: (x, y) → (−x, −y)
- That's a rotation of 180° about the origin.

**SAY:** "Sometimes there are multiple correct answers. A 180° rotation is one. You could also do: reflect over x-axis, then reflect over y-axis. Both work."

---

### [0:47 - 0:53] COMMON TRAPS

**TRAP 1 — CW vs CCW:**
"90° clockwise = 270° counterclockwise. Some textbooks say one, some say the other, but exams use both. They're the same transformation."

---

**TRAP 2 — Composition order:**
"Always apply transformations LEFT to RIGHT in the order stated. If I say 'reflect then translate', you do the reflection FIRST, then the translation. Not the other way."

---

**TRAP 3 — Dilation and congruence:**
"If a problem asks 'are these figures congruent?' and you needed a dilation to map one to the other — the answer is NO. They're similar. Congruence requires rigid motions ONLY. No dilations."

---

**TRAP 4 — Reflection over y=x vs y=−x:**
"y = x: swap the coordinates → (a, b) → (b, a)"
"y = −x: swap AND negate → (a, b) → (−b, −a)"
"Mix these up and you'll get the wrong answer every time. Draw the line if you have to."

#### TRIANGLE ANGLE SUM THEOREM

**SAY:** "The three interior angles of ANY triangle add to 180°. Always. No matter the shape, size, orientation. 180°."

**PROBLEM 25:**
"A triangle has angles 2x, 3x, and 40°. Find x and all three angles."

**Walk through:**

- 5x + 40 = 180
- 5x = 140
- x = 28
- Angles: 2(28) = 56°, 3(28) = 84°, 40°
- Check: 56 + 84 + 40 = 180 ✓

---

#### EXTERIOR ANGLE THEOREM

**DRAW:** A triangle with one side extended, creating an exterior angle at the extension.

**SAY:** "An exterior angle of a triangle equals the SUM of the two remote interior angles. Remote means they're the two angles NOT adjacent to the exterior angle."

---

**PROBLEM 26:**
"In triangle ABC, ∠A = 45° and ∠B = 70°. Find the exterior angle at C."

**Walk through:**
- Exterior angle at C = ∠A + ∠B = 45 + 70 = 115°
- (Also, interior ∠C = 180 − 45 − 70 = 65°, and the exterior angle should be 180 − 65 = 115° ✓)

---

**PROBLEM 27:**
"The exterior angle at vertex C is (5x + 10)°. The two remote interior angles are (2x + 5)° and (x + 25)°. Find x."

**Walk through:**

- 5x + 10 = (2x + 5) + (x + 25)
- 5x + 10 = 3x + 30
- 2x = 20
- x = 10
- Remote interior: 2(10) + 5 = 25°, 10 + 25 = 35°
- Exterior: 5(10) + 10 = 60°
- Check: 25 + 35 = 60 ✓

#### TRIANGLE CLASSIFICATION

#### ISOSCELES TRIANGLE THEOREM

**SAY:** "If two sides of a triangle are congruent, then the angles OPPOSITE those sides are also congruent. Those opposite angles are called the BASE ANGLES."

---

**PROBLEM 28:**
"In isosceles triangle ABC, AB = AC and ∠A = 50°. Find ∠B and ∠C."

**Walk through:**
- If AB = AC, then the angles opposite them are equal: ∠C = ∠B (the base angles)
- ∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180
- 50 + ∠B + ∠B = 180 (since ∠C = ∠B)
- 50 + 2∠B = 180
- 2∠B = 130
- ∠B = 65° = ∠C

**SAY:** "This theorem is gold. Whenever you see equal sides, the opposite angles are equal."

### [0:15 - 0:35] BLOCK 2 — The 5 Congruence Theorems (THIS IS THE CORE)

**SAY:** "Two triangles are CONGRUENT if they're the exact same shape and size. To prove it, you don't need to check all 6 parts (3 sides + 3 angles). You just need the RIGHT COMBINATION. There are exactly 5 valid combinations. If you know these 5, you can prove any two triangles congruent."

**For each one, DRAW two triangles and mark the congruent parts:**

---

#### SSS (Side-Side-Side)

**SAY:** "If all three pairs of corresponding sides are congruent, the triangles are congruent."

**DRAW:** Two triangles with all three sides marked with tick marks (matching ticks).

**SAY:** "When to spot it: you're given three side measurements or you see three pairs of tick marks. No angles needed."

---

#### SAS (Side-Angle-Side)

**SAY:** "Two pairs of sides AND the angle BETWEEN them (the included angle) are congruent."

**DRAW:** Two triangles with two sides and the angle between them marked.

**CRITICAL:** "The angle MUST be between the two sides. If it's not between them, this doesn't work. The angle has to be INCLUDED."

---

#### ASA (Angle-Side-Angle)

**SAY:** "Two pairs of angles AND the side BETWEEN them (the included side) are congruent."

**DRAW:** Two triangles with two angles and the side between them marked.

---

#### AAS (Angle-Angle-Side)

**SAY:** "Two pairs of angles AND a side that is NOT between them. This works because if two angles match, the third angle must match too (since they add to 180°). So you effectively have all three angles matching, plus a side."

**DRAW:** Two triangles with two angles and a non-included side marked.

---

#### HL (Hypotenuse-Leg)

**SAY:** "This one is ONLY for right triangles. If both triangles are right triangles and the hypotenuse and one leg are congruent, then the triangles are congruent."

**DRAW:** Two right triangles (with right angle marked) with hypotenuse and one leg marked.

**SAY:** "This is basically a consequence of the Pythagorean theorem. If the hypotenuse and one leg match, the other leg must match too."

---

#### SSA — The One That DOES NOT WORK

**SAY:** "SSA — two sides and a non-included angle — does NOT prove congruence. Why? Because given two sides and an angle not between them, you can sometimes construct TWO different triangles. It's ambiguous. There are two possible positions for the third vertex."

**DRAW:** Show the ambiguous case — same two sides and angle, but the third side can swing to two different positions.

**SAY:** "If someone tells you SSA on an exam, the answer is 'not enough information.' Period. Don't use it."

---

### DRILL — Rapid Identification (Quick Practice)

### [0:35 - 0:50] BLOCK 3 — Proofs and CPCTC

**SAY:** "Now the part everyone dreads: proofs. But honestly, they're just systematic. Every congruence proof follows the same flow."

**WRITE the flow on screen:**
```
1. State what's Given
2. Find congruent parts (using given, definitions, theorems)
3. Conclude triangles are congruent (state the theorem: SSS/SAS/ASA/AAS/HL)
4. If you need specific parts to be congruent → use CPCTC
```

**SAY:** "CPCTC stands for 'Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent.' It means: AFTER you've proven two triangles congruent, you can say ANY of their corresponding parts are congruent. It's always the LAST step."

---

#### EXAMPLE PROOF — Walk Through Together

**WRITE the problem on screen:**
```
Given: AB̅ ≅ CD̅, and BC̅ ≅ DA̅.
Quadrilateral ABCD has diagonal AC̅ drawn.
Prove: ∠B ≅ ∠D.
```

**DRAW:** Quadrilateral ABCD with diagonal AC drawn. Label the vertices.

**SAY:** "Let's build this proof step by step."

**WRITE on screen:**

| # | Statement | Reason |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 1 | AB ≅ CD | Given |
| 2 | BC ≅ DA | Given |
| 3 | AC ≅ AC | Reflexive Property |
| 4 | △ABC ≅ △CDA | SSS (statements 1, 2, 3) |
| 5 | ∠B ≅ ∠D | CPCTC |

**SAY:** "See step 3? AC is SHARED by both triangles. That's the Reflexive Property — a segment is congruent to itself. This shows up in almost every proof. Look for shared sides."

"And step 5 is CPCTC — we already proved the triangles congruent in step 4, so now we can conclude any corresponding parts are congruent. In this case, angle B from the first triangle corresponds to angle D from the second."

---

#### SECOND PROOF — They Lead, You Guide

**WRITE on screen:**
```
Given: ∠1 ≅ ∠2, and AE̅ ≅ CE̅.
Point E is the intersection of AC̅ and BD̅.
Prove: BE̅ ≅ DE̅.
```

**DRAW:** Two triangles with diagonals intersecting at E. Mark angles 1 and 2 at E.

**GUIDE them step by step:**

"What do we know?"
→ ∠1 ≅ ∠2 (given), AE ≅ CE (given)

"What else can we figure out?"
→ ∠AEB ≅ ∠CED (they're vertical angles when lines cross)

"Which theorem can we use?"
→ ASA (we have angle-side-angle)

"So what?"
→ △AEB ≅ △CED by ASA

"Now apply CPCTC."
→ The corresponding parts are equal, specifically BE ≅ DE ✓

**SAY:** "See the pattern? Given info → find the third piece (often by recognizing vertical angles or reflexive property) → name the theorem → use CPCTC. Same flow every time."

---

#### TIPS FOR PROOFS

**SAY:** "Here are things that help:"

- "Always mark up the diagram first with everything you know. Use tick marks for equal sides and angle arcs for equal angles."
- "Look for shared sides — that's the Reflexive Property."
- "Look for vertical angles when lines cross — those are always equal."
- "If there are parallel lines mentioned, look for alternate interior angles."
- "The reason is NEVER 'because it looks like it' — every step needs a named theorem or definition. That's the whole thing about proofs."

### [0:15 - 0:35] BLOCK 2 — The Quadrilateral Hierarchy (MOST IMPORTANT PART)

**SAY:** "This is the single most important thing in this unit. If you understand the hierarchy, you understand everything."

**DRAW the tree on screen:**

```
              QUADRILATERAL
              /            \
         TRAPEZOID        KITE
             |
       PARALLELOGRAM
        /          \
   RECTANGLE     RHOMBUS
        \          /
          SQUARE
```

**SAY:** "Everything flows DOWNWARD. A square is a rectangle. A square is a rhombus. A rectangle is a parallelogram. A parallelogram is a trapezoid. Properties flow down — anything true about a parallelogram is ALSO true about rectangles, rhombi, and squares."

---

#### PARALLELOGRAM (Spend the most time here)

**SAY:** "A parallelogram has FOUR key properties. Learn these cold:"

**WRITE and EXPLAIN:**

1. **"Opposite sides are PARALLEL and CONGRUENT"**
   - Side AB is parallel to side CD, and AB = CD
   - Side BC is parallel to side DA, and BC = DA

2. **"Opposite angles are CONGRUENT"**
   - ∠A = ∠C and ∠B = ∠D

3. **"Consecutive angles are SUPPLEMENTARY (add to 180°)"**
   - ∠A + ∠B = 180°, ∠B + ∠C = 180°, etc.

4. **"Diagonals BISECT each other"**
   - The diagonals cut each other in half at the intersection point

---

**PROBLEM 40:**
"Parallelogram ABCD. ∠A = 3x + 10, ∠B = 5x − 30. Find all four angles."

**Walk through:**
- Consecutive angles are supplementary: (3x + 10) + (5x − 30) = 180
- 8x − 20 = 180
- 8x = 200
- x = 25
- ∠A = 3(25) + 10 = 85°
- ∠B = 5(25) − 30 = 95°
- ∠C = 85° (opposite to A)
- ∠D = 95° (opposite to B)
- Check: 85 + 95 + 85 + 95 = 360 ✓

---

**PROBLEM 41:**
"The diagonals of parallelogram ABCD intersect at E. AE = 3x + 2, EC = 5x − 6. Find AC."

**Walk through:**
- Diagonals bisect each other: AE = EC
- 3x + 2 = 5x − 6
- 8 = 2x
- x = 4
- AE = 3(4) + 2 = 14, EC = 5(4) − 6 = 14
- AC = AE + EC = 14 + 14 = 28

#### RECTANGLE

**SAY:** "A rectangle is a parallelogram with all right angles. It gets ALL parallelogram properties PLUS one extra: the diagonals are CONGRUENT."

**PROBLEM 42:**

---

#### RHOMBUS

**SAY:** "A rhombus is a parallelogram with all sides congruent. It gets ALL parallelogram properties PLUS two extras: (1) diagonals are perpendicular, (2) diagonals bisect the angles."

---

**PROBLEM 43:**
"Rhombus ABCD has diagonals of length 10 and 24. Find the side length."

**Walk through:**
- Diagonals bisect each other at right angles
- Half of diagonal 1 = 10/2 = 5
- Half of diagonal 2 = 24/2 = 12
- Each side is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 5 and 12
- Side = √(5² + 12²) = √(25 + 144) = √169 = 13

---

#### SQUARE

**SAY:** "A square is BOTH a rectangle AND a rhombus. It has every single property of both. All sides congruent, all angles 90°, diagonals congruent, perpendicular, bisect each other, and bisect the angles."

#### KITE

### [0:35 - 0:45] BLOCK 3 — Coordinate Proofs for Quadrilaterals

**SAY:** "On exams, they'll give you four vertices and ask you to determine the most specific name for the quadrilateral. Here's the strategy:"

**WRITE:**
```
1. Plot the points (optional but helpful)
2. Find ALL four side lengths (distance formula)
   → check for congruent sides
3. Find ALL four slopes
   → check for parallel sides (same slope)
   → check for perpendicular sides (negative reciprocal slopes)
4. If needed: find midpoints of diagonals
   → check if they bisect each other
5. Classify using the hierarchy
```

---

**PROBLEM 45:**
"Classify the quadrilateral with vertices A(0, 0), B(4, 3), C(8, 0), D(4, −3)."

**Walk through together:**

Step 1: Find side lengths (distance formula)
- AB = √[(4−0)² + (3−0)²] = √(16+9) = √25 = 5
- BC = √[(8−4)² + (0−3)²] = √(16+9) = √25 = 5
- CD = √[(4−8)² + (−3−0)²] = √(16+9) = √25 = 5
- DA = √[(0−4)² + (0−(−3))²] = √(16+9) = √25 = 5

**SAY:** "All sides are 5. So it's at least a rhombus."

Step 2: Find slopes (to check for parallel sides and perpendicularity)
- Slope AB = (3−0)/(4−0) = 3/4
- Slope BC = (0−3)/(8−4) = −3/4
- Slope CD = (−3−0)/(4−8) = −3/−4 = 3/4
- Slope DA = (0−(−3))/(0−4) = 3/−4 = −3/4

**SAY:** "AB ∥ CD (both slope 3/4), BC ∥ DA (both slope −3/4). That confirms it's a parallelogram."

Step 3: Check if it's a rectangle
- Are consecutive sides perpendicular?
- AB slope × BC slope = (3/4) × (−3/4) = −9/16 ≠ −1, so NOT perpendicular
- So it's NOT a rectangle

**FINAL ANSWER:** "RHOMBUS (not a square because the angles aren't 90°)"

**SAY:** "See the process? Distance tells you about side lengths. Slope tells you about parallel and perpendicular. Use all three tools."
