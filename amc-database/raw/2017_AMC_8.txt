=== Problem 10 ===
A box contains five cards, numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Three cards are selected randomly without replacement from the box. What is the probability that 4 is the largest value selected?

Choices: {'A': '\\frac{1}{10}', 'B': '\\frac{1}{5}', 'C': '\\frac{3}{10}', 'D': '\\frac{2}{5}', 'E': '\\frac{1}{2}</math>'}
Answer: C

--- Solution 1 ---
There are <imath>\binom{5}{3}</imath> possible groups of cards that can be selected. If <imath>4</imath> is the largest card selected, then the other two cards must be either <imath>1</imath>, <imath>2</imath>, or <imath>3</imath>, for a total <imath>\binom{3}{2}</imath> groups of cards. Then, the probability is just <imath>{\frac{{\dbinom{3}{2}}}{{\dbinom{5}{3}}}} = \boxed{{\textbf{(C) }} {\frac{3}{10}}}</imath>.

--- Solution 2 ---
P (no 5)= <imath>\frac{4}{5}</imath> * <imath>\frac{3}{4}</imath> * <imath>\frac{2}{3}</imath> = <imath>\frac{2}{5}</imath>. This is the fraction of total cases with no fives.
p (no 4 and no 5)= <imath>\frac{3}{5}</imath> * <imath>\frac{2}{4}</imath> * <imath>\frac{1}{3}</imath> = <imath>\frac{6}{60}</imath> = <imath>\frac{1}{10}</imath>. This is the intersection of no fours and no fives. Subtract the fraction of no fours and no fives from that of no fives. <imath>\frac{2}{5} - \frac{1}{10} = \frac{3}{10} = \boxed{{\textbf{(C) }} {\frac{3}{10}}}</imath>.

--- Solution 3 ---
Pick any desirable situation. Let's say you first draw a 4, then a 3, then a 2. The probability of that is <imath>\frac{1}{5}</imath> * <imath>\frac{1}{4}</imath> * <imath>\frac{1}{3}</imath>, which is <imath>\frac{1}{60}</imath>. And for this case, it doesn't matter if you first draw a 3 or a 2. The probability of drawing a 4, a 3, and a 2 in any order is <imath>\frac{1}{60}</imath>. 

Next, list other desirable situations. You can draw a 4, 3, 1, or a 4,2,1, regardless of order. So, our desirable situations are 4,3,2, then 4,3,1, and lastly 4,2,1, regardless of order.

Now, for each desirable situation, there are 3!=6 ways to arrange the numbers. That means that there are 6 ways to arrange 4,3,1, then 6 ways to arrange 4,2,1, and 6 ways to arrange 4,3,2. 6*3=18, which means, accounting for order, there are 18 desirable outcomes. 

The probability of each desirable outcome is <imath>\frac{1}{60}</imath>. Since there are 18 of them, the probability that 4 is the highest value selected is <imath>\frac{1}{60}</imath>*18, which equals <imath>\frac{18}{60}</imath>, which simplifies to the answer,<imath>\frac{3}{10}</imath>.

=== Problem 11 ===
A square-shaped floor is covered with congruent square tiles. If the total number of tiles that lie on the two diagonals is 37, how many tiles cover the floor?

Choices: {'A': '148', 'B': '324', 'C': '361', 'D': '1296', 'E': '1369</math>'}
Answer: C

--- Solution 1 ---
Since the number of tiles lying on both diagonals is <math>37</math>, counting one tile twice, there are <math>37=2x-1\implies x=19</math> tiles on each side, where x is the number of tiles on the side length of the square. This is because the number of tiles on the square's diagonal is equal to the number of tiles on the square's side length.Therefore, our answer is <math>19^2=\boxed{\textbf{(C)}\ 361}</math>.

We can do this problem by computing how many ways there are to get to each letter (in order). There is <math>1</math> way to get to the <math>A</math> in the center. * For the last <imath>5</imath> days, there are <imath>2</imath> days that Mrs. Sanders does not receive a phone call, as indicated by the first <imath>5</imath> days in a cycle.

Together, the answer is <imath>24\cdot6+2=\boxed{\textbf{(D) }146}.</imath>

--- Solution 3 ---
For any randomly chosen day, there is a <math>\frac{2}{3}</math> chance the first child does not call her, a <math>\frac{3}{4}</math> chance the second child does not call her and a <math>\frac{4}{5}</math> chance the third child does not call her. So, in a randomly chosen day, there is a <math>\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{2}{5}</math> chance no child calls her.

In particular, this "unrigorous" reasoning becomes rigorous, by linearity of expectation, when we take a sample of <math>360</math> days; over the first <math>360</math> days, <math>360 \times \frac{2}{5} = 144</math> days will go without a phone call. Now, we simply check the remaining days; on day <math>361</math> and <math>362</math>, nobody calls her; on day <math>363</math>, the child that calls every three days will call her; on day <math>364</math>, the child that calls every four days will call her; on day <math>365</math>, the child that calls every five days will call her. Thus, we add two more days to <math>144</math>, to get our answer of <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D) }146}</math>.
