Answer: D

--- Solution 1 ---
By a modulo <imath>10</imath> argument, the ones digits repeat with period <imath>10</imath> in the following order:
<cmath>1,4,9,6,5,6,9,4,1,0</cmath>
The sum of the numbers can be verified to be <imath>45</imath>.

There are <imath>202</imath> periods that occur from <imath>1</imath> to <imath>2025</imath>, and there are five extra numbers, those being <imath>1,4,9,6,5</imath>, corresponding to <imath>2021,2022,2023,2024,2025</imath>. The sum of these numbers is <imath>25</imath>.

Hence, the total is
<cmath>202\cdot 45+25=9090+25=\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 2 ---
Let <imath>x\equiv n\quad\textrm{(mod 10)}</imath>. We have <imath>x^2\equiv n^2\quad\textrm{(mod 10)}</imath>. So, the numbers must repeat in patterns of <imath>10</imath>. Listing them out gives:
<cmath>1,4,9,6,5,6,9,4,1,0.</cmath>These numbers sum to <imath>45</imath>, so the sum of the first <imath>2020</imath> numbers is <imath>45\cdot202=9090</imath>. Since we need <imath>5</imath> more numbers, we add <imath>1+4+9+6+5=25</imath> to our answer to get <imath>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 3 ---
The ones digit of a square number is determined by the ones digit of the base.  
We can find the pattern by looking at the ones digits of the first ten positive squares:


\begin{aligned}
1^2 &= 1 \\
2^2 &= 4 \\
3^2 &= 9 \\
4^2 &= 16 \quad (\text{ones digit } 6) \\
5^2 &= 25 \quad (\text{ones digit } 5) \\
6^2 &= 36 \quad (\text{ones digit } 6) \\
7^2 &= 49 \quad (\text{ones digit } 9) \\
8^2 &= 64 \quad (\text{ones digit } 4) \\
9^2 &= 81 \quad (\text{ones digit } 1) \\
10^2 &= 100 \quad (\text{ones digit } 0)
\end{aligned}


The sequence of ones digits is:


<imath>1</imath>, <imath>4</imath>, <imath>9</imath>, <imath>6</imath>, <imath>5</imath>, <imath>6</imath>, <imath>9</imath>, <imath>4</imath>, <imath>1</imath>, <imath>0</imath> 



This pattern of ten digits repeats for every subsequent group of ten squares (e.g., \(11^2\) has the same ones digit as \(1^2\), \(12^2\) as \(2^2\), etc.).


<imath>1</imath> + <imath>4</imath> + <imath>9</imath> + <imath>6</imath> + <imath>5</imath> + <imath>6</imath> + <imath>9</imath> + <imath>4</imath> + <imath>1</imath> + <imath>0</imath> = <imath>45</imath> 


We now divide by <imath>10</imath> as the pattern repeats every <imath>10</imath> terms: <imath>2025</imath>/<imath>10</imath> results in <imath>202</imath> full cycles with a remainder of <imath>5</imath>. The sum of the <imath>202</imath> full cycles is:


<imath>202</imath> * <imath>45</imath> = <imath>9090</imath> 


However, we must add back <imath>5</imath> digits, as we had a remainder of <imath>5</imath> when we divided <imath>2025</imath> by <imath>10</imath>. Therefore, the sum of the remaining <imath>5</imath> digits is:


<imath>1</imath> + <imath>4</imath> + <imath>9</imath> + <imath>6</imath> + <imath>5</imath> = <imath>25</imath> 


Thus, the total sum is:


<imath>9090</imath> + <imath>25</imath> = <imath>9115</imath> 

Therefore, the answer is <imath>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

==Solution 4: Modular Arithmetic; Ultra-Rigorous, Not Recommended==

We want the sum of the ones digits of 
\( 1^2, 2^2, 3^2, \ldots, 2025^2 \). The normal way is to notice the pattern repeats every 10, but instead we will approach this with more modular arithmetic than any sane person would tolerate.

Let \( f(n) = n^2 \mod 10 \), which extracts the ones digit of \( n^2 \).
We want

<cmath>
S = \sum_{n=1}^{2025} f(n).
</cmath>

First observe the basic periodicity result:
Since \( n^2 \mod 10 \) depends only on \( n \mod 10 \), we have

<cmath>
f(n+10) \equiv f(n) \pmod{10}.
</cmath>

We will pretend this is deep.

Now compute all residues \( n \mod 10 \) and their corresponding \( n^2 \mod 10 \):

<cmath>
\begin{array}{c c}
0^2 & \equiv 0 \\
1^2 & \equiv 1 \\
2^2 & \equiv 4 \\
3^2 & \equiv 9 \\
4^2 & \equiv 6 \\
5^2 & \equiv 5 \\
6^2 & \equiv 6 \\
7^2 & \equiv 9 \\
8^2 & \equiv 4 \\
9^2 & \equiv 1.
\end{array}
</cmath>

Instead of immediately summing these, we treat the 10-term sequence as a modular function over the ring \( \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z} \).
Define the sequence \( a_k = (k^2 \mod 10) \) for \( k \in \mathbb{Z}/10\mathbb{Z} \).
We want the sum

<cmath>
T = \sum_{k=0}^9 a_k = 0 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 6 + 5 + 6 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 45.
</cmath>

Next, determine how many full cycles of length 10 lie in the first 2025 integers.
Compute

<cmath>
2025 \mod 10 \equiv 5.
</cmath>

Then

<cmath>
2025 = 10 \cdot 202 + 5.
</cmath>

So we have 202 complete cycles plus 5 extra terms.

Sum contributed by the full cycles:

<cmath>
202 \cdot 45.
</cmath>

We now compute this entirely modulo 10 even though we need the actual sum.
First,

<cmath>
202 \equiv 2 \pmod{10}, \quad 45 \equiv 5 \pmod{10},
</cmath>

so

<cmath>
202 \cdot 45 \equiv 2 \cdot 5 = 10 \equiv 0 \pmod{10}.
</cmath>

This tells us nothing useful, so abandon that and compute normally:

<cmath>
202 \cdot 45 = (200 \cdot 45) + (2 \cdot 45) = 9000 + 90 = 9090.
</cmath>

Now handle the leftover 5 terms: \( n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 \).
Compute \( n^2 \mod 10 \) again even though we already have it:

<cmath>
1^2 \equiv 1, \quad 2^2 \equiv 4, \quad 3^2 \equiv 9, \quad 4^2 \equiv 6, \quad 5^2 \equiv 5.
</cmath>

Sum:

<cmath>
1 + 4 + 9 + 6 + 5 = 25.
</cmath>

The full sum is

<cmath>
S = 9090 + 25 = 9115.
</cmath>

To unnecessarily verify using modular decomposition, write

<cmath>
S \equiv \sum_{n=1}^{2025} n^2 \pmod{10}.
</cmath>

We can compute the entire sum of squares formula modulo 10 and compare.
Using

<cmath>
\sum_{n=1}^N n^2 = \frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}{6},
</cmath>

take \( N = 2025 \).
Reduce everything modulo 10 first:

<cmath>
2025 \equiv 5, \quad 2026 \equiv 6, \quad 4051 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}.
</cmath>

Now compute

<cmath>
5 \cdot 6 \cdot 1 = 30.
</cmath>

Thus

<cmath>
\sum_{n=1}^{2025} n^2 \equiv 30 \cdot 6^{-1} \pmod{10}.
</cmath>

Compute the inverse of 6 modulo 10.
Solve \( 6x \equiv 1 \pmod{10} \).
No such \( x \) exists because \( \gcd(6, 10) = 2 \neq 1 \).
Therefore this modular-verification method is unusable, so we immediately stop and simply trust the earlier cycle-counting method.

Thus the sum of the ones digits of the first 2025 squares is

<cmath>
9115.
</cmath>

By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, it suffices to find <imath>6^{6^6}\pmod{4}</imath> and <imath>6^{6^6}\pmod{25}</imath> and find the solution to the system. We know that <imath>6^{6^6}\equiv 0\pmod{4}</imath>, so we just need to find the remainder modulo <imath>25</imath>.

By Euler’s Totient Theorem, <imath>6^{6^6}\equiv 6^r\pmod{25}</imath>, where <imath>r\equiv 6^6\pmod{\varphi(25)=20}</imath>. Hence we need to find <imath>6^6\pmod{20}</imath>.

Now we again use the Chinese Remainder Theorem. We know that <imath>6^6\equiv 0\pmod{4}</imath> and <imath>6^6\equiv 1^6\equiv 1\pmod{5}</imath>. Testing cases yields <imath>6^6\equiv 16\pmod{20}</imath>.

Therefore, we know that <imath>6^{6^6}\equiv 6^{16}\pmod{25}</imath>. Now we can simplify:
<cmath>6^{6^6}\equiv 6^{16}\equiv (6^4)^4\equiv 1296^4\equiv (-4)^4\equiv 256\equiv 6\pmod{25}</cmath>
Solving the system <imath>6^{6^6}\equiv 6\pmod{25}</imath> and <imath>6^{6^6}\equiv 0\pmod{4}</imath> yields <imath>6^{6^6}\equiv 56\pmod{100}</imath>. Hence the answer is <imath>\boxed{\textbf{(C)}

--- Solution 1 ---
We will arrange the nine athletes in a line, with the first three being in the blue group, the next three being in the red group, and the last three being in the green group. Suppose the three tallest athletes are named <imath>A, B, C</imath>, in some order. 

We have <imath>3!</imath> ways to choose which group each of these athletes can be in. We then have <imath>3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3</imath> ways to determine which of the three positions in that group they can take. From here, we must distribute the remaining six athletes in the remaining six spaces, which can be done in <imath>6!</imath> ways.

There are therefore <imath>6 \cdot 27 \cdot 6!</imath> favorable outcomes. There are also <imath>9!</imath> total ways to arrange the athletes with no restrictions. Our answer is <imath>\frac{6 \cdot 27 \cdot 6!}{9!} = \frac{6 \cdot 27}{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7} = \boxed{\textbf{(C) }\frac{9}{28}}.</imath>

--- Solution 2 ---
Let the three tallest athletes be <imath>A_1</imath> (tallest), <imath>A_2</imath> (second-tallest), and <imath>A_3</imath> (third-tallest).

Athlete <imath>A_2</imath> (2nd Tallest): <imath>A_2</imath> must draw a band of a different color than <imath>A_1</imath>.

<imath>A_1</imath> took one band, so 2 bands of that color remain.

Awnik repeatedly plays a game that has a probability of winning of

Answer: D

--- Solution 1 ---
Let the probability of a win be <imath>p</imath> and the probability of a loss be <imath>q</imath>. If the first game is a win, then we must find the expected number of further games to get a loss, which will be <imath>\frac{1}{q}</imath>. In addition to the first game played, there will be <imath>1+\frac{1}{q}</imath> games played. Therefore, the expected number of games needed to get a win on game <imath>1</imath> and then a loss is <imath>(p)\left(1+\frac{1}{q}\right).</imath>

Similarly, if the first game is a loss, we need to the find the expected number of further games to get a win, which will be <imath>\frac{1}{p}</imath>. There will be a total of <imath>1+\frac{1}{p}</imath> games played. Therefore, the expected number of games needed to get a loss on game <imath>1</imath> and then a win is <imath>(q)\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).</imath>

The answer is
<cmath>(p)\left(1+\frac{1}{q}\right) + (q)\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)</cmath>
<cmath>\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) \left(1+\frac{3}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)(1+3) = \frac{5}{6} + \frac{8}{3} = \boxed{\frac{7}{2}}.</cmath>

--- Solution 2 ---
Note that if we stop after getting at least one win and one loss, then the stopping condition is the first state change. If we start with a win, then we count the expected number of rounds until we lose and vice versa.

We use first-step analysis:
<cmath>\mathbb{E}[T]=1+\sum_{s\in \Omega}\mathbb{P}(s)\mathbb{E}[T-1|s]</cmath>
Here, we have <imath>\mathbb{P}(W)=\frac{1}{3}, \mathbb{P}(L)=\frac{2}{3}</imath>.

Using first-step analysis on each condition expectation, by the Markov property
<cmath>\mathbb{E}[T|W]=1+\mathbb{P}(W)\mathbb{E}[T|W]+\mathbb{P}(L)(0)=1+\mathbb{P}(W)\mathbb{E}[T|W]</cmath>
Solving, <imath>\mathbb{E}[T|W]=\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{P}(W)}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(L)}</imath>. Similarly, <imath>\mathbb{E}[T|L]=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(W)}</imath>. Write <imath>p=\mathbb{P}(W)</imath>. <imath>q=\mathbb{P}(L)=1-p</imath>.
<cmath>\mathbb{E}[T]=1+\mathbb{P}(W)\mathbb{E}[T|W]+\mathbb{P}(L)\mathbb{E}[T|L]=1+p\left( \frac{1}{q} \right)+q\left( \frac{1}{p} \right)=1+\frac{p}{q}+\frac{q}{p}=1+\frac{p^2+q^2}{pq}</cmath>
We can simplify using <imath>(p+q)^2=1=p^2+q^2+2pq</imath>, giving <imath>p^2+q^2=1-2pq</imath>. Therefore,
<cmath>\mathbb{E}[T]=1+\frac{1-2pq}{pq}=1+\frac{1}{pq}-2=\frac{1}{pq}-1</cmath>
Substituting <imath>p=\frac{1}{3}</imath>, <imath>q=\frac{2}{3}</imath> yields <imath>3\cdot \frac{3}{2}-1=\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \frac{7}{2}}</imath>.

3 games played: 3 * ((2/3)^2 * 1/3 + (1/3)^2 * 2/3) = 18/27

Since <imath>\angle AOA’=\angle AA’O=60^{\circ}</imath>, <imath>\angle AA’C=120^{\circ}</imath>, then use Law of Cosines in <imath>\triangle AA’C</imath>:
<cmath>s^{2}=AC^{2}=1^{2}+2^{2}-2\cdot 1\cdot{2}\cdot\cos 120^{\circ}=\boxed{\textbf{(E)} \ 7}</cmath>

--- Solution 5 ---
Let <imath>O</imath> be the common center and let the vertices of the triangle <imath>A,B,C</imath> satisfy <imath>OA=1</imath>, <imath>OB=2</imath>, <imath>OC=3</imath>, with side length <imath>AB=BC=CA=s</imath>. Let <imath>x=s^{2}</imath>. Let <imath>\alpha=\angle AOB</imath>, <imath>\beta=\angle BOC</imath>, <imath>\gamma=\angle COA</imath>, so <imath>\alpha+\beta+\gamma=360^\circ</imath>. By the Law of Cosines,

<cmath>x = 1^2+2^2-2\cdot1\cdot2\cos\alpha = 5-4\cos\alpha \Rightarrow \cos\alpha = \frac{5-x}{4}</cmath>
<cmath>x = 2^2+3^2-2\cdot2\cdot3\cos\beta = 13-12\cos\beta \Rightarrow \cos\beta = \frac{13-x}{12}</cmath>
<cmath>x = 3^2+1^2-2\cdot3\cdot1\cos\gamma = 10-6\cos\gamma \Rightarrow \cos\gamma = \frac{10-x}{6}</cmath>

For angles summing to <imath>360^\circ</imath>, we have

<cmath>\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma - 2\cos\alpha\cos\beta\cos\gamma = 1</cmath>
Substitute <imath>\cos\alpha,\cos\beta,\cos\gamma</imath> in terms of <imath>x</imath> and plug in answer choices (don't bother trying to solve it), we get <imath>x = \boxed{7}</imath>.

--- Solution 6 ---
Let <imath>\omega_1</imath> be a circle of radius <imath>1</imath> centered at <imath>O=(0, 0)</imath>. Let <imath>\omega_2</imath> be a circle of radius <imath>2</imath> centered at <imath>(0, 0)</imath>. Let <imath>A</imath> be the point <imath>(0, 3)</imath>. Let <imath>B</imath> be some point on <imath>\omega_1</imath>. Let <imath>C</imath> be some point in the plane such that <imath>ABC</imath> is equilateral. Our goal is to find some <imath>C</imath> that lies on <imath>\omega_2</imath>. 

Because <imath>\angle BAC=60^\circ</imath> and <imath>AB=AC</imath>, we see that <imath>C</imath> must be the rotation of <imath>B</imath> around <imath>A</imath>, either <imath>60^\circ</imath> clockwise or counterclockwise. Note that because the locus of all points <imath>B</imath> is <imath>\omega_1</imath>, the locus of all points <imath>C</imath> is the two circles formed by rotating <imath>\omega_1</imath> <imath>60^\circ</imath> around <imath>A</imath>, either direction. 

We want a <imath>C</imath> that lies on <imath>\omega_2</imath>, so we naturally intersect <imath>\omega_2</imath> with one of these two circles (I chose the one that's rotated counterclockwise because the numbers are nicer). We could easily calculate the intersections using coord geo, but notice that the problem asks for a unique <imath>s</imath>; this means that the circle is tangent to <imath>\omega_2</imath>. The tangent point, which is the only <imath>C</imath> that works, lies on the line connecting the centers. The center (denote it <imath>O'</imath>) of (half of) <imath>C</imath>'s locus is the center of <imath>\omega_2</imath> rotated <imath>60^\circ</imath> counterclockwise. Because <imath>AO=AO'</imath> and <imath>\angle OAO'=60^\circ</imath>, we see <imath>\triangle OAO'</imath> is equilateral, meaning <imath>\angle AOO'=\angle AOC=60^\circ</imath>, which means by extension that <imath>C</imath> is <imath>30^\circ</imath> counterclockwise from the horizontal. <imath>OC=2</imath> as <imath>C</imath> lies on <imath>\omega_2</imath>, so the coordinates of <imath>C</imath> are at <imath>(2\cos 30^\circ, 2\sin 30^\circ)=(\sqrt{3}, 1)</imath>. <imath>A</imath> is at <imath>(0, 3)</imath>, and a quick distance formula gives us <imath>s^2=(C.x-A.x)^2+(C.y+A.y)^2=3+4=\boxed{7}</imath>.
