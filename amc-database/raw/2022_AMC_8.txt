=== Problem 6 ===
Three positive integers are equally spaced on a number line. The middle number is

Choices: {'A': '4', 'B': '5', 'C': '6', 'D': '7', 'E': '8</math>'}
Answer: C

--- Solution 1 ---
Let the smallest number be <math>x.</math> It follows that the largest number is <math>4x.</math>

Since <math>x,15,</math> and <math>4x</math> are equally spaced on a number line, we have
<cmath>\begin{align*}
4x-15 &= 15-x \\
5x &= 30 \\
x &= \boxed{\textbf{(C) } 6}.
\end{align*}</cmath>

--- Solution 2 ---
Let the common difference of the arithmetic sequence be <math>d</math>. Consequently, the smallest number is <math>15-d</math> and the largest number is <math>15+d</math>. As the largest number is <math>4</math> times the smallest number, <math>15+d=60-4d\implies d=9</math>. Finally, we find that the smallest number is <math>15-9=\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 6}</math>.

--- Solution 3 ---
Let the smallest number be <math>x</math>. Because <math>x</math> and <math>4x</math> are equally spaced from <math>15</math>, <math>15</math> must be the average. By adding <math>x</math> and <math>4x</math> and dividing by <math>2</math>, we get that the mean is also <math>2.5x</math>. We get that <math>2.5x=15</math>, and solving gets <math>x=\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 6}</math>.

--- Solution 4 ---
Since the problem is giving us the options, we can try all of them. The differences MUST be equal because the numbers have to be evenly spaced on the number line. We see that 4 cannot work because 15 - 4 = 11, and 4 * 4 = 16. 16 - 15 is not 11, so this option does not work. Next, we can try 5. We see that this also does not work because 15 - 5 = 10, and 5 * 4 = 20. 20 - 15 = 5, and this is not 10. We can then try 6 to see that it does work. 15 - 6 = 9, and 6 * 4 = 24. 24 - 15 = 9. The differences are equivalent, so we can see that 6 indeed does work. Therefore, the answer is <math>\boxed{\textbf{(C)}


=== Problem 7 ===
When the World Wide Web first became popular in the

Choices: {'A': '0.6', 'B': '10', 'C': '1800', 'D': '7200', 'E': '36000'}
Answer: B

--- Solution 1 ---
Notice that the number of kilobits in this song is <math>4.2 \cdot 8000 = 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 100.</math>

We must divide this by <math>56</math> in order to find out how many seconds this song would take to download: <math>\frac{\cancel{8}\cdot\cancel{7}\cdot6\cdot100}{\cancel{56}} = 600.</math>

Finally, we divide  this number by <math>60</math> because this is the number of <i><b>seconds</b></i> to get the answer <math>\frac{600}{60}=\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 10}.</math>

--- Solution 2 ---
We seek a value of <math>x</math> that makes the following equation true, since every other quantity equals <math>1</math>.

<cmath>\frac{x\ \text{min}}{4.2\ \text{mb}} \cdot \frac{56\ \text{kb}}{1\ \text{sec}} \cdot \frac{1\ \text{mb}}{8000\ \text{kb}} \cdot \frac{60\ \text{sec}}{1\ \text{min}} = 1.</cmath>
Solving yields <math>x=\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 10}</math>.

--- Solution 1 ---
Let <imath>A</imath> denote the leaf where the cricket starts and <imath>B</imath> denote one of the other <imath>3</imath> leaves. Note that:

* If the cricket is at <imath>B,</imath> then the probability that it hops to <imath>A</imath> next is <imath>\frac13.</imath>

* If the cricket is at <imath>B,</imath> then the probability that it hops to <imath>B</imath> next is <imath>\frac23.</imath>

We apply casework to the possible paths of the cricket:
<ol style="margin-left: 1.5em;">

</ol>
Together, the probability that the cricket returns to <imath>A</imath> after <imath>4</imath> hops is <imath>\frac19+\frac{4}{27}=\boxed{\textbf{(E) }\frac{7}{27}}.</imath>

==Remark==

This problem is a reduced version of [https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php/1985_AIME_Problems/Problem_12 1985 AIME Problem 12], changing <imath>7</imath> steps into <imath>4</imath> steps.

This problem is also similar to [https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php/2003_AIME_II_Problems/Problem_13 2003 AIME II Problem 13].


\begin{solution}
At each hop, the cricket must move to one of the other three leaves with equal probability.
Thus the total number of possible hop sequences of length <imath>4</imath> is
\[
3^4 = 81.
\]

We count the number of \emph{favorable} sequences in which the cricket is on the starting
leaf after the fourth hop.

---

\textbf{Case 1: The cricket returns to the starting leaf exactly once.}

Since the cricket cannot remain on the same leaf, the only way for it to return exactly once
and still end at the starting leaf is for the return to occur on the \emph{fourth hop}.

\begin{itemize}
\item Hop 1: From the starting leaf, there are <imath>3</imath> choices.
\item Hop 2: The cricket is on a non-starting leaf; it must avoid returning, so there are <imath>2</imath> choices.
\item Hop 3: Again, the cricket is on a non-starting leaf and must avoid returning, giving <imath>2</imath> choices.
\item Hop 4: The cricket must return to the starting leaf, which is <imath>1</imath> favorable choice.
\end{itemize}

Thus the number of favorable sequences in Case 1 is
\[
3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 12.
\]

---

\textbf{Case 2: The cricket returns to the starting leaf exactly twice.}

With four hops and no ability to stay in place, the only possible return pattern is
\[
\text{start} \to \text{other} \to \text{start} \to \text{other} \to \text{start},
\]
which corresponds to hops <imath>2</imath> and <imath>4</imath> being returns.

\begin{itemize}
\item Hop 1: From the starting leaf, there are <imath>3</imath> choices.
\item Hop 2: The cricket must return to the starting leaf (<imath>1</imath> choice).
\item Hop 3: From the starting leaf, there are again <imath>3</imath> choices.
\item Hop 4: The cricket must return to the starting leaf (<imath>1</imath> choice).
\end{itemize}

Thus the number of favorable sequences in Case 2 is
\[
3 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 9.
\]

---

\textbf{Conclusion.}

The total number of favorable sequences is
\[
12 + 9 = 21.
\]
Therefore, the desired probability is
\[
\frac{21}{81} = \boxed{\frac{7}{27}}.
\]
\end{solution}
