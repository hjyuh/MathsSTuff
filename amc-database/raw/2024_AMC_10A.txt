Choices: {'A': '~2', 'B': '~20', 'C': '~200', 'D': '~202', 'E': '~2020'}
Answer: A

--- Solution 1 ---
The likely fastest method will be direct computation. <math>9901\cdot101</math> evaluates to <math>1000001</math> and <math>99\cdot10101</math> evaluates to <math>999999</math>. The difference is <math>\boxed{\textbf{(A) }2}.</math>

--- Solution 2 ---

<cmath>\begin{align*}

&= 10000\cdot101-99\cdot101-99\cdot10000-99\cdot101 \\
&= (10000\cdot101-99\cdot10000)-2\cdot(99\cdot101) \\
&= 2\cdot10000-2\cdot9999 \\
&= \boxed{\textbf{(A) }2}.
\end{align*}</cmath>

--- Solution 3 ---
Note that <math>9901\cdot101=9901\cdot100+9901=990100+9901=1000001</math> and  <math>99\cdot10101=100\cdot10101-10101=1010100-10101=999999</math>, therefore the answer is <math>1000001-999999=\boxed{\textbf{(A) }2}</math>.

--- Solution 4 ---

Because answer <math>\textbf{(D)}</math> is <math>202=2\cdot 101</math>, that cannot be the answer, so we choose choice <math>\boxed{\textbf{(A) }2}</math>.

Since the only answers with <imath>2</imath> in the units digit are <imath>\textbf{(A)}</imath> and <imath>\textbf{(D)}</imath>, we can then continue if you are desperate to use guess and check or an actually valid method to find the answer is <imath>\boxed{\textbf{(A) }2}</imath>.

=== Problem 6 ===
What is the minimum number of successive swaps of adjacent letters in the string

Choices: {'A': '~6', 'B': '~10', 'C': '~12', 'D': '~15', 'E': '~24'}
Answer: D

--- Solution 1 ---
Procedurally, it takes:

* <math>5</math> swaps for <math>A</math> to move to the sixth spot, giving <math>BCDEFA.</math>

* <math>4</math> swaps for <math>B</math> to move to the fifth spot, giving <math>CDEFBA.</math>

* <math>3</math> swaps for <math>C</math> to move to the fourth spot, giving <math>DEFCBA.</math>

* <math>2</math> swaps for <math>D</math> to move to the third spot, giving <math>EFDCBA.</math>

* <math>1</math> swap for <math>E</math> to move to the second spot (so <math>F</math> becomes the first spot), giving <math>FEDCBA.</math>

Together, the answer is <math>5+4+3+2+1=\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 2 ---
We can proceed by a recursive tactic on the number of letters in the string.

Looking at the string <math>A</math>, there are <math>0</math> moves needed to change it to the string <math>A</math>

Then, there is <math>1</math> move to change <math>AB</math> to <math>BA</math>.

Similarly, there is <math>3</math> moves needed for three letters (said in the problem).

There are <math>6</math> moves needed to change <math>ABCD</math> to <math>DCBA</math>.

We see a pattern of <math>0,1,3,6,...</math>. We notice that the difference between consecutive terms is increasing by <math>1</math>, so in the same way, for <math>5</math> letters, we would need <math>10</math> moves, and for <math>6</math>, we would need <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}
Thinking why, when we start making these moves, we see that for a string of length <math>n</math>, it takes <math>n-1</math> moves to move the last letter to the front. After, we get a string that will be changed identically to a string of length <math>n-1</math>. This works in our pattern above and is another way to think about the problem!


Note: 

The sequence consists of triangular numbers shifted a term up (as it starts with <math>0</math> on term <math>1</math> and <math>1</math> on term <math>2</math>.)
Thus, our explicit formula is <cmath>\dfrac{(n-1)(n+1-1)}{2} = \dfrac{(n)(n-1)}{2}</cmath> and as <math>n = 6</math> in this case (<math>6</math> letters), our answer is <math>\dfrac{(6)(6-1)}{2} = \boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 3 ---
We can see that the most efficient way to change <math>ABCDEF</math> to <math>FEDCBA</math> is the same as changing <math>ABCDE</math> to <math>EDCBA</math> and then moving <math>F</math> to the front in <math>5</math> moves. Similarly, this would carry on downwards, where to change <math>ABCDE</math> to <math>EDCBA</math> would be to change <math>ABCD</math> to <math>DCBA</math> and move <math>E</math> <math>4</math> times to the front. This pattern will carry on until <math>AB</math> to <math>BA</math> would be <math>1</math>, and <math>A</math> to <math>A</math> would be <math>0</math>. The answer would be <math>0(A)+1(B)+2(C)+3(D)+4(E)+5(F)</math>, which is <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 4 ---
Simply, just blindly doing it, the most straightforward way is to shift F all the way back. From ABCDEF:

<math>ABCDFE ->ABCFDE ->ABFCDE ->AFBCDE ->FABCDE</math>

For E:

<math>FABCED -> FABECD -> FAEBCD -> FEABCD</math>

For D:

<math>FEABDC -> FEADBC -> FEDABC</math>

For C:

<math>FEDACB -> FEDCAB</math>

For B:

FEDCBA

That's it, you don't need to do it with A. Still <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 5 ---
Let the letters <math>A, B, C, D, E, F</math> correspond to the numbers <math>1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6</math> respectively. We want to find the number of swaps required to transform the permutation <math>1 2 3 4 5 6</math> into the permutation <math>6 5 4 3 2 1</math>. 

Here, we let <math>1 2 3 4 5 6</math> be the natural order. Then the total number of inversions of the permutation <math>6 5 4 3 2 1</math> is <math>1+2+3+4+5=15</math>. Hence the answer is <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

=== Problem 8 ===

--- Solution 1 ---
Note that Amy, Bomani, and Charlie pack a total of <math>4+3+3=10</math> packages every <math>3</math> minutes.

The total amount of time worked is <math>1</math> hour and <math>45</math> minutes, which when converted to minutes, is <math>105</math> minutes. This means that since Amy, Bomani, and Charlie worked for the entire <math>105</math> minutes, they in total packed <math>\dfrac{105}{3}\cdot10=350</math> packages.

Since <math>450</math> packages were packed in total, then Daria must have packed <math>450-350=100</math> packages in total, and since she packs at a rate of <math>5</math> packages per <math>4</math> minutes, then Daria worked for <math>\dfrac{100}{5}\cdot4=80</math> minutes, therefore Daria joined <math>80</math> minutes before <math>2:45</math> PM, which was at <math>\boxed{\textbf{(A) }1:25\text{ PM}}</math>

--- Solution 2 ---
Let the time, in minutes, elapsed between <math>1:00</math> and the time Daria joined the packaging be <math>x</math>. Since Amy packages <math>4</math> packages every <math>3</math> minutes, she packages <math>\frac{4}{3}</math> packages per minute. Solving this equation, we get <math>x=25</math>, meaning Daria arrived <math>25</math> minutes after <math>1:00</math>, meaning the answer is <math>\boxed{\textbf{(A) }1:25\text{ PM}}</math>.


=== Problem 9 ===

--- Solution 1 ---
The number of ways in which we can choose the juniors for the team are <math>{6\choose2}{4\choose2}{2\choose2}=15\cdot6\cdot1=90</math>. Thus, the total number of ways in which we can choose two juniors and two seniors for three distinguishable teams is <math>225\cdot 36 = 8100</math> ways. === Problem 11 ===
How many ordered pairs of integers

Choices: {'A': '~1', 'B': '~2', 'C': '~3', 'D': '~4', 'E': '~\\text{infinitely many}'}
Answer: D

--- Solution 1 ---
Note that <math>m</math> is a nonnegative integer.

We square, rearrange, and apply the difference of squares formula to the given equation: <cmath>(n+m)(n-m)=49.</cmath>
It is clear that <math>n+m\geq n-m,</math> so <math>(n+m,n-m)=(49,1),(7,7),(-7,-7),(-1,-49).</math> Each ordered pair <math>(n+m,n-m)</math> gives one ordered pair <math>(m,n),</math> so there are <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}
<u><b>Remark</b></u>

From <math>(n+m,n-m)=(49,1),(7,7),(-7,-7),(-1,-49),</math> we get <math>(m,n)=(24,25),(0,7),(0,-7),(24,-25),</math> respectively.

--- Solution 2 ---
Squaring both sides of the given equation gives <cmath>n^2-49=m^2\rightarrow n^2-m^2=49\rightarrow (n+m)(n-m)=49</cmath> Splitting <math>49</math> into its factors (keep in mind it doesn't ask for positive integers, so the factors can be double negative, too) gives six cases:

<math>(1\cdot49)</math>

<math>(7\cdot7)</math>

<math>(49\cdot1)</math>

<math>(-1\cdot -49)</math>

<math>(-7\cdot -7)</math>

<math>(-49\cdot -1)</math>.

Note that the square root in the problem doesn't have <math>\pm</math> with it. Therefore, if there are two solutions, <math>(n,m)</math> and <math>(n,-m)</math>, then these together are to be counted as one solution.
The solutions expressed as <math>(n,m)</math> are:

<math>(25,24)</math>

<math>(25,-24)</math>

<math>(7,0)</math>

<math>(-7,0)</math>

<math>(-25,24)</math>

<math>(-25,-24)</math>.

<math>(25,24)</math> and <math>(25,-24)</math> are to be counted as one, same for <math>(-25,24)</math> and <math>(-25,-24)</math>. Therefore, the solution is <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

--- Solution 3 ---
From looking at the problem, it's obvious that <math>(\pm7,0)</math> are already solutions. From squaring and rearranging, we get <cmath>n^2-m^2=49.</cmath> We know that the difference between two consecutive squares is always odd, and for each pair of increasing consecutive squares, the difference starts from <math>3</math> and increases by <math>2</math> each time. This means that there is an existing pair, <math>(\pm n,m)</math> of consecutive squares that will satisfy this equation. 

Also note that the answer cannot be infinity because the difference between two squares will increase as the two squares get higher, consecutive or not.

Therefore, the solutions <math>(\pm7,0)</math> and <math>(\pm n,m)</math> where <math>n</math> and <math>m</math> are consecutive that have a square difference of <math>49,</math> give the answer of <math>\boxed{\textbf{(D)}

We also know the perpendicular distance from the circle’s center \((0,12)\) to \(f(x)\) must be \(12\), because the circle of radius \(12\) is externally tangent to that line.  The distance from a point \(\bigl(x_0,y_0\bigr)\) to a line \(Ax + By + C = 0\) is: 

<cmath>\frac{|Ax_0 + By_0 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}.</cmath>

Here, \(f(x) = -\sqrt{3}\,x + c\) can be rewritten as \(\sqrt{3}\,x + y - c = 0.\)  Plugging in \((x_0,y_0)=(0,12)\) and setting the distance to 12 yields:

<cmath>\frac{|\sqrt{3}\cdot 0 \;+\; 12 \;-\; c|}{\sqrt{(\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2}}\;=\;12.</cmath>

<cmath>f(x) \;=\; -\sqrt{3}\,x \;-\; 12.</cmath>

<cmath>12\sin(\theta) + 12 \;=\; -\sqrt{3}\,\bigl(12\cos(\theta)\bigr) - 12.</cmath>

<cmath>\frac{x}{\sqrt{3}} + 12 \;=\; -\sqrt{3}\,x - 12,</cmath>

Below is a sample drawing.

Roll 1: From <imath>(0,0,0)</imath>, there are 3 favorable outcomes that can occur. They are listed below.

[[File:Q242.png | center | 650 px]]

[[File:Q243.png | center | 650px]]

Roll 4: Finally, WLOG, say the bee moves to the left one. From that point, there are still 2 ways the bee can go.

The total probability for one cube is <imath>\frac{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2}{6^4} \Rightarrow \frac{1}{54} </imath>. We have 8 of these cubes, which gives us a total probability of <imath> \frac{8}{54} </imath>.

However, we have overcounted. Notice that if the bee moved down one instead of left one at roll 3, it would only have one way. This is because the bee has a choice between either the origin or a unique point. This will always occur on the third move, and because we have 8 cubes(The 3d graph has one for each quadrant), the probability of this happening is <imath> \frac{3 \cdot 8}{6^4} = \frac{1}{54} </imath>*.

Our probability is <imath> \frac{8}{54} - \frac{1}{54} = </imath> <imath>\boxed{\textbf{(B) }\frac{7}{54}}</imath>.


===*===
One might question this logic here, onto why it is that we multiply the moves by the number of cubes over the number of rolls.

The theory comes from expected value. It is certain that the bee will be selective in a situation where it only has one choice every 3 moves. This is due to the figure being a cube. Then, the expected probability of this happening is the moves multiplied by the single probability that this case appears, which is <imath>\frac{1}{6^4}</imath>. I put this as an experiment. If anything feels off or you don't understand, do not worry. This is very high end mathematics, and it is fine to feel confused. Solution 1 and the below solutions are much easier than this.

Here, we explore the topological (study of bendable figures in space) definition of this problem.

Consider an \( n \)-dimensional torus that the bee travels upon. Then, this torus must be an \( 8 \times 8 \times 8 \), as there are \( 8 \) three-dimensional cubes. The reason we can relate this to a torus is due to the physique of the \( 3D \) plane. 

Then, we wish to find the ways to get from \( (0,0,0) \) to \( (0,0,0) \) in \( 512 \) moves. That isn't too difficult. In topology, the number of ways to do this is 

<cmath>
\sum_{\gcd(x,y,z,8) = 1} \binom{8}{x} \binom{8}{y} \binom{8}{z}
</cmath>

Summing over all values \( x, y, \) and \( z \) gives \( 2^{21} \times 7 \).

Now, the total. The total number of moves that exist are all \( 512 \) plausible moves upon each probable space such that not all \( x, y, z \) are in \( \gcd \) relation of \( 1 \) with \( 8 \). There is then \( 3^{512} \) different ways to permute \( x, y, \) and \( z \). However, not all of these are plausible, as \( x + y + z = 512 \). These coordinates then force their way back to the origin due to the fact \( x \equiv y \equiv z \equiv 0 \pmod{8} \). Then, we can continue with roots of unity to filter out these multiples of \( 8 \). Our standard divisibility counter must satisfy 

<cmath>
\mathbf{1}_{x \equiv 0 \pmod{8}} = \frac{1}{8} \sum_{j=0}^7 \omega^{jx}, \quad \omega = e^{2\pi i / 8}
</cmath>

(Recall that this bolded 1 is the indicator function).

Application to all coordinates satisfies 

<cmath>
N = \frac{1}{8^3} \sum_{a,b,c=0}^{7} \sum_{x+y+z=512} \binom{512}{x, y, z} \omega^{ax+by+cz}
</cmath>

Through the [[Multinomial Theorem]], 

<cmath>
\sum_{x+y+z=512} \binom{512}{x, y, z} \omega^{ax+by+cz} = (\omega^a + \omega^b + \omega^c)^{512}
</cmath>

is simplified, and thus we obtain 

<cmath>
N = \frac{1}{512} \sum_{a,b,c=0}^{7} (\omega^a + \omega^b + \omega^c)^{512}
</cmath>

Because 512 is divisible by 8, any term where \( |\omega^a + \omega^b + \omega^c| < 2 \) doesn't contribute to the overall terms \( x \), \( y \), and \( z \). The only triplets that count are magnitude 2.

Carrying this out gives \( 3^3 \) different full unital root contributions, and these are applied in \( 2^{22} \) different roots, for a total \( 2^{22} \times 27 \) ways. 

Our probability is then
<cmath>
\frac{2^{21} \times 7}{2^{22} \times 27} = \frac{7}{2 \times 27} = \boxed{\frac{7}{54}}
</cmath>

Once again, this is an incredibly difficult solution. Before even attempting this, be sure you have a solid background in intermediate to Olympiad level algebra, precalculus, and counting/probability.

--- Solution 4 ---
Remove the dice. It is not necessary for this problem. It is the same problem if you instead view this problem as the bee moving one up or down in the <imath>x, y,</imath> and <imath>z</imath> directions.

Count the total number of ways for one cube of the 8 total ones. It is not hard to see that after the first step (which you can travel to <imath>(1,0,0), (0,1,0),</imath> or <imath>(0,0,1)</imath> -  3 ways), you have a total of 8 moves to traverse a face of that 1x1 cube. Hence, for each cube, you would have <imath>8 \cdot 3 = 24</imath> total moves.

There are a total of 8 cubes you could have traversed, though, so multiplying this by <imath>8</imath> yields <imath>8 \cdot 8 \cdot 3.</imath>

We have to account for overcounting. For example, when we traversed the face in the <imath>xz</imath> plane <imath>(0,0,0), (0,0,1),(1,0,0),(1,0,1),</imath> the number of ways that we traverse that particular face got counted twice, once from the cube in the far upper right and once from the cube in the closer upper right.

For a given face, you can go clockwise or counterclockwise, hence yielding <imath>2</imath> ways. There are a total of <imath>12</imath> shared faces, each one shared by exactly two faces so no additonal PIE is needed. We simply subtract <imath>2\cdot12</imath> from our original count now, yielding <imath>8 \cdot 3 \cdot 8 - 2 \cdot 12 = 168.</imath> 

With no restrictions, there are a total of <imath>6^4</imath> ways to move four steps. Your probability is <imath>\frac{168}{6^4}=\frac{7}{54}.</imath>

--- Solution 5 ---
An invariant is that the sum of all three coordinates change parity with every roll of the dice. Hence, to end up on a vertex of a unit cube, it can only be <imath>(0,0,0)</imath>, <imath>(1,1,0)</imath>, <imath>(1,0,1)</imath>, <imath>(0,1,1)</imath>, and all the negative permutations of each listed (in total, <imath>13</imath> different final coordinates can be reached).

For <imath>(0,0,0)</imath>, we choose two of the three coordinates to have 'cancelling' operations. Let these be <imath>a</imath>, <imath>a'</imath>, <imath>b</imath>, <imath>b'</imath> (where <imath>a</imath> cancels out <imath>a'</imath>, hence cannot be placed next to each other, which would be traversing the same edge). There are <imath>3\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=24</imath> ways for this (we can only have forms of <imath>aba'b'</imath> or <imath>bab'a'</imath> and their permutations among <imath>a</imath> and <imath>b</imath>).

For <imath>(1,1,0)</imath>, we must have two operations <imath>a</imath>, <imath>b</imath> and a pair <imath>c</imath>, <imath>c'</imath>. Experimenting with having <imath>3</imath> of the same type of operation leads to finding a repeated edge no matter the arrangement. These can be arranged in <imath>12</imath> ways (place the operations <imath>a</imath> and <imath>b</imath> first, placing the operations <imath>c</imath> and <imath>c'</imath> in the remaining spaces).

Analogously, <imath>(-1,1,0)</imath>, <imath>(1,-1,0)</imath>, <imath>(-1,-1,0)</imath> all have the same number of ways, <imath>12</imath> each (they are merely a rotation of all possibilities of <imath>(1,1,0)</imath>). Furthermore, we can generalise this to all the <imath>8</imath> negative permutations of <imath>(1,0,1)</imath>, <imath>(0,1,1)</imath>, for a total of <imath>12\cdot4\cdot3=144</imath>. 

So, <imath>\frac{24+144}{6^4}=\frac{7\cdot24}{54\cdot24}=\frac{7}{54}</imath>, hence we pick <imath>\boxed{\textbf{(B) }\frac{7}{54}}</imath>.

Observations: 

1. You can not have a vertical line in any place other than the first two columns and the last two columns. If we did, we would have at least one of the middle cells with toothpicks along more than one side, which would violate the conditions of the problem.

2. There are two cases that look completely different. We can have a long horizontal box that spans all eight cells either on top of or below the middle cells, or we have to have a shape that looks like a rectangle, except with a few places "pushed" in. 

Thus, using casework, we can split the task of finding those rectangles with squiggly edges into 3 cases.

For case 1, we assume that the green lines shown below are given (always have toothpicks on them). In effect, we will use all eight columns.

Now, concentrate on the first row of squares. A toothpick can be placed on either the bottom or top and connected to a continuous squiggle by adding vertical toothpicks:

How many squiggles are possible?
