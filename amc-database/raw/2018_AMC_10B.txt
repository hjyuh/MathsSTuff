--- Solution 2 ---
Note that the obtuse angle in the triangle has to be opposite the side that is always length <imath>1</imath>. This is because the largest angle is always opposite the largest side, and if two sides of the triangle were <imath>1</imath>, the last side would have to be greater than <imath>1</imath> to make an obtuse triangle. Using this observation, we can set up a law of cosines where the angle is opposite <imath>1</imath>:

<cmath>1^2=x^2+y^2-2xy\cos(\theta)</cmath>

where <imath>x</imath> and <imath>y</imath> are the sides that go from <imath>[0,1]</imath> and <imath>\theta</imath> is the angle opposite the side of length <imath>1</imath>.

By isolating <imath>\cos(\theta)</imath>, we get:

<cmath>\frac{1-x^2-y^2}{-2xy} = \cos(\theta)</cmath>

For <imath>\theta</imath> to be obtuse, <imath>\cos(\theta)</imath> must be negative. Therefore, <imath>\frac{1-x^2-y^2}{-2xy}</imath> is negative. Since <imath>x</imath> and <imath>y</imath> must be positive, <imath>-2xy</imath> must be negative, so we must make <imath>1-x^2-y^2</imath> positive. From here, we can set up the inequality
<cmath>x^2+y^2<1</cmath>

Notice, the area of the convex hexagon formed through the intersection of the <math>2</math> triangles can be found by finding the area of the triangle formed by the midpoints of the sides and subtracting the smaller triangles that are formed by the region inside this triangle but outside the other triangle. First, let's find the area of the triangle formed by the midpoint of the sides. Notice, this is an equilateral triangle, thus all we need is to find the length of its side.

===Lemma 1: <math>MP</math> is midsegment of <math>\triangle ANE</math>.===
As <math>ZX</math> is a midsegment of trapezoid <math>FEBA</math>, we know that <math>FA||ZX||EB</math>. Furthermore, by subdividing trapezoid <math>FEBA</math>, <math>ZM,MP,PX</math> are midsegments of <math>\triangle EFA, \triangle ANE, \triangle, ANB</math> respectively.

===Lemma 2: <math>[ACE] = \frac12[ABCDEF]</math>===
Due to radial symmetry about the center, <math>G</math>, we only have to prove <math>[AGC] = [CBA]</math>. As <math>\triangle AGB</math> and <math>\triangle CBG</math> are equilateral triangles, <math>AC</math> therefore divides rhombus <math>ABCG</math> into 2 congruent halves. <math>\triangle AGC</math> and <math>\triangle CBA</math> thus each contain two such congruent triangles, which means that their areas are equal.

===Lemma 3: <math>[AMP] = \frac1{16}[ABCDEF]</math>===
