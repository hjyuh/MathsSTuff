# Calculus 1–3 Self-Study Guide

A curated collection of resources for teaching yourself single-variable and multivariable calculus. Covers free and paid options across video lectures, textbooks, class notes, problem sets, visual/intuition builders, and interactive tools.

---

## How to use this guide

The honest truth: you don't need *all* of these. Pick a **spine** (one main video course + one main textbook) and use the rest as supplements when a topic doesn't click. A common winning combo:

- **Spine:** Professor Leonard (videos) + OpenStax or Stewart (textbook) + Paul's Online Notes (reference)
- **Intuition layer:** 3Blue1Brown's *Essence of Calculus* before each major topic
- **Drill layer:** Schaum's or Demidovich for problems
- **Rigor layer (optional, after Calc 1 basics):** Spivak or Apostol if you want proofs

Calc 1 = limits, derivatives, integrals (single variable). Calc 2 = integration techniques, sequences/series, parametric/polar. Calc 3 = vectors, partial derivatives, multiple integrals, vector calculus.

---

## 1. Recommended path (if you only want one)

1. Watch **3Blue1Brown — *Essence of Calculus*** (~3 hours) for big-picture intuition before you start.
2. Work through **Professor Leonard's Calculus 1 → 2 → 3** YouTube playlists as your main lectures.
3. Read the matching chapter in **OpenStax Calculus Vol. 1, 2, 3** (free PDFs) before/after each lecture.
4. Use **Paul's Online Math Notes** as your written reference and for extra worked examples.
5. Drill problems from **Schaum's Outline of Calculus** (cheap, 1,100+ solved problems).
6. Verify computations with **Wolfram Alpha** and visualize with **Desmos / GeoGebra 3D**.

That sequence alone, done seriously, will get you fluent in Calc 1–3.

## 3. Textbooks

### Free (full PDFs, legal)

- **OpenStax Calculus Volumes 1, 2, 3** by Strang & Herman — clean, modern, well-illustrated, used at many universities. [Volume 1](https://openstax.org/details/books/calculus-volume-1) — change the volume number for 2 and 3.
- **Strang's *Calculus*** (MIT) — older, free, characteristically Strang. Available via the [MIT OCW open textbook page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-18-001-calculus-fall-2023/).
- **Active Calculus** (Boelkins, Austin, Schlicker) — free, problem-driven, designed for active learning. activecalculus.org
- **APEX Calculus** — free, well-typeset, used at some service academies.
- **Paul Dawkins's notes (Lamar)** — really a book in disguise, free PDFs.

### Paid standards (the "standard" college experience)

- **Stewart, *Calculus: Early Transcendentals*** — by far the most-used U.S. college textbook. Computational, lots of problems, clear. Get an older edition (8th, 9th) cheap.
- **Thomas, *Calculus*** — long-running alternative to Stewart, similar in style.
- **Larson, *Calculus*** — another mainstream option, slightly easier reading.
- **Anton, *Calculus*** — the gentlest of the mainstream three.

### Paid rigorous (proof-based, "real" calculus)

- **Spivak, *Calculus*** — beautifully written, conversational, builds calculus from the real numbers up. Single-variable only. The most-loved rigorous calc book; pairs well with a problem solutions manual.
- **Apostol, *Calculus* Vol. I & II** — more formal than Spivak. Vol. I integrates first then differentiates (unusual order). Vol. II covers multivariable + a real linear algebra introduction. Probably the best problem sets of any calc text.
- **Courant & John, *Introduction to Calculus and Analysis*** — classic, deep, physically motivated. Old-school but excellent.
- **Hubbard & Hubbard, *Vector Calculus, Linear Algebra, and Differential Forms*** — a *fantastic* rigorous treatment of multivariable calculus; covers Calc 3 + linear algebra + an introduction to differential forms.

### Calc 3 specifically

- **Marsden & Tromba, *Vector Calculus*** — solid, traditional Calc 3 text.
- **Colley, *Vector Calculus*** — clean, well-liked alternative.
- **Hubbard & Hubbard** (above) — the rigorous choice.

### Pleasure reading / unusual

- **Silvanus Thompson, *Calculus Made Easy*** (1910, free online) — charming, opinionated, "What one fool can do, another can." Read it for the vibes.
- **Tristan Needham, *Visual Complex Analysis*** and *Visual Differential Geometry* — not Calc 1–3, but gives a sense of where this all eventually goes.

## 5. Problem books (the most underrated layer)

You learn calculus by doing thousands of problems, not by watching lectures. Pick one or two and grind.

- **Schaum's Outline of Calculus** (Ayres & Mendelson) — 1,100+ solved problems, ~$15.
- **Schaum's 3,000 Solved Problems in Calculus** — exactly what it says.
- **Demidovich, *Problems in Mathematical Analysis*** — Russian classic. Thousands of problems with answers/hints. [Free archive PDF](https://archive.org/details/DemidovichEtAlProblemsInMathematicalAnalysisMir1970).
- **Berkeley Problems in Mathematics** (de Souza & Silva) — past Berkeley qualifying exams; harder, mostly post-calculus, but the analysis sections are worth seeing.
- **Putnam and Beyond** (Andreescu & Gelca) — competition problems, includes a deep calculus section.
- **Spivak's exercises** — if you're using Spivak, the exercises *are* the course. A solutions manual exists.
- **Apostol's exercises** — similarly, the heart of the book.

## 7. Interactive tools

- **[Desmos](https://www.desmos.com/calculator)** — graphing calculator in the browser. Use sliders to see how a function changes; build your own visualizations.
- **[Desmos 3D](https://www.desmos.com/3d)** — for Calc 3 surfaces.
- **[GeoGebra](https://www.geogebra.org/3d)** — also excellent for 3D plots, vector fields, parametric surfaces.
- **[Wolfram Alpha](https://www.wolframalpha.com/)** — type any integral or derivative and get step-by-step solutions (paid feature, ~$5/mo for Pro). Also free for plain answers.
- **[SymPy](https://www.sympy.org/)** in Python — free symbolic math; great if you want to verify computations programmatically.
- **[Symbolab](https://www.symbolab.com/)** — competitor to Wolfram, also has step-by-step.
- **[CalcPlot3D](https://c3d.libretexts.org/CalcPlot3D/index.html)** — purpose-built Calc 3 visualization tool.
- **[Paul Falstad's Vector Field applets](https://www.falstad.com/vector/)** — instant intuition for div, curl, gradient.

## 8. Topic checklist (use to verify you've covered the territory)

### Calculus 1 — Differentiation & basic integration

Limits and continuity (epsilon-delta optional but recommended). Derivatives: definition, rules (product, quotient, chain), implicit differentiation, derivatives of trig/exp/log/inverse trig. Applications: related rates, optimization, linear approximation, Newton's method, l'Hôpital. Mean Value Theorem. Antiderivatives, definite integrals, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, u-substitution, basic area/volume.

### Calculus 2 — Integration & series

Techniques of integration: integration by parts, trig integrals, trig substitution, partial fractions, improper integrals. Applications of integration: arc length, surface area, volumes by shells/disks, work, center of mass, probability. Sequences and series: convergence tests (ratio, root, comparison, integral, alternating), power series, Taylor and Maclaurin series, radius/interval of convergence. Parametric equations and polar coordinates: derivatives, area, arc length.

### Calculus 3 — Multivariable & vector calculus

Vectors and 3D geometry: dot/cross product, lines, planes, quadric surfaces. Vector functions and curves: derivatives, arc length, curvature, tangent/normal/binormal. Functions of several variables: limits, partial derivatives, tangent planes, linearization, chain rule, directional derivatives, gradient, optimization (critical points, second-derivative test, Lagrange multipliers). Multiple integrals: double integrals (Cartesian and polar), triple integrals (Cartesian, cylindrical, spherical), change of variables, Jacobians. Vector fields: line integrals, conservative fields and potential functions, Green's theorem, surface integrals, Stokes' theorem, the divergence theorem.

## 9. Study tips that actually matter

Watch a lecture, then *immediately* do problems on the same topic — passive watching alone barely sticks. Aim for problems before notes; the lecture lands harder once you've already struggled. Re-derive formulas from scratch a week later; if you can't, you don't know it yet. Keep a single notebook (paper or digital) where you write down every "trick" — the moment something clicks. Get unstuck fast: if you're stuck >20 minutes, hit Paul's Notes or Wolfram Alpha for a hint, then redo the problem from scratch the next day. Old MIT/Berkeley/Harvard exams are the single best practice; they're free.

## 10. After Calc 3

Natural next stops: Linear Algebra (3Blue1Brown's *Essence of Linear Algebra* + Strang's *Linear Algebra and its Applications* or Axler's *Linear Algebra Done Right*), Differential Equations (Strogatz's *Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos* for intuition; Boyce & DiPrima for the standard course; MIT 18.03), Real Analysis (Abbott's *Understanding Analysis* is the friendliest entry; Rudin if you want pain), Probability and Statistics, or jumping into application areas (physics, ML, signal processing).
