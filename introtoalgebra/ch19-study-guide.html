19.1 — Exponential Functions

Core Idea

In an expression like \(a^x\), when \(a\) is a constant greater than 1 and \(x\) is the variable, we call \(f(x) = a^x\) an exponential function. These functions grow incredibly fast — that's exponential growth.

The Rice on the Chessboard

Two girls choose rewards from a king:

  • Arial's deal (linear): 1 grain on day 1, then 10 more each day. Her function is \(A(n) = 10n - 9\). Growth by a constant amount.
  • Meena's deal (exponential): 1 grain on day 1, doubling each day. Her function is \(M(n) = 2^{n-1}\). Growth by a constant factor.

At first Arial gets more, but by day 7 Meena overtakes her. By day 21 Meena receives over 1 million grains. Exponential growth always eventually dominates linear growth.

Key facts about \(f(x) = 2^x\):
Domain: all real numbers (you can input any \(x\))
Range: all positive numbers (\(2^x\) is never zero or negative)
As \(x \to -\infty\), the function approaches 0 but never reaches it.
As \(x\) increases, the function "explodes" upward.

Solving Exponential Equations

Strategy 1: Match the bases. If \(a^m = a^n\) and \(a > 1\), then \(m = n\).

Example: Solve \(3^{2x} = 3^{x-5}\).
Bases are the same, so set exponents equal: \(2x = x - 5\), giving \(x = -5\).

Strategy 2: Rewrite everything with the same base.

Example: Solve \(2^{(16^x)} = 16^{(2^x)}\).
Since \(16 = 2^4\), rewrite as \(2^{(2^4)^x} = (2^4)^{(2^x)}\).
Use \((a^b)^c = a^{bc}\) to get \(2^{4x} = 2^{2+x}\).
Match exponents: \(4x = 2 + x\), so \(x = 2/3\).

Strategy 3: Substitution. When variables are stuck in exponents, substitute \(y = a^x\) to create a familiar equation.

Example: Solve \(4^x - 33 \cdot 2^{x-1} + 8 = 0\).
Rewrite: \(2^{2x} - \tfrac{33}{2}\cdot 2^x + 8 = 0\).
Let \(y = 2^x\). Then \(y^2 - \tfrac{33y}{2} + 8 = 0\), so \(2y^2 - 33y + 16 = 0\).
Factor: \((2y - 1)(y - 16) = 0\), giving \(y = 1/2\) or \(y = 16\).
Back-substitute: \(2^x = 1/2 \Rightarrow x = -1\), and \(2^x = 16 \Rightarrow x = 4\).
Warning — Don't confuse these two rules:
\((a^b)^c = a^{bc}\) — power of a power: multiply exponents
\((a^b)(a^c) = a^{b+c}\) — product of same base: add exponents

Real-World Application: Carbon Dating

Carbon-14 has a half-life of ~5700 years. The equation \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^t = \frac{a}{b}\) tells you how many half-lives have passed (where \(a\) = current amount, \(b\) = original amount). The fossil's age is \(5700t\) years.

19.2 — Show Me the Money

Simple Interest

Interest is charged only on the original principal (the amount borrowed/invested). You add the same dollar amount each year — this is linear.

Simple Interest Formula: After \(n\) years at rate \(r\%\) on principal \(\$k\): \[\text{Total} = \left(1 + \frac{nr}{100}\right)(\$k)\]

Compound Interest

Interest is charged on the total amount (principal + accumulated interest). Each year you multiply by the same factor — this is exponential.

Compound Interest (Annually): After \(n\) years at rate \(r\%\) on principal \(\$k\): \[\text{Total} = \left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^n (\$k)\]

Key insight: The difference between simple and compound interest is the same as the difference between addition and multiplication. Simple interest adds the same amount each year. Compound interest multiplies by the same factor each year.

Compounding Multiple Times per Year

If interest compounds \(m\) times per year:

General Compound Interest: Rate \(r\%\), compounded \(m\) times/year, for \(n\) years: \[\text{Total} = \left(1 + \frac{r}{100m}\right)^{nm} (\$k)\]
Example: \(\$10{,}000\) at 14% compounded quarterly (\(m = 4\)) for 1 year:
\(\left(1 + \frac{0.14}{4}\right)^4 (\$10{,}000) = (1.035)^4 (\$10{,}000) \approx \$11{,}475.23\)

The Number \(e\)

As you compound more and more frequently, the total approaches a limit connected to the constant:

\[e = \lim_{n \to \infty}\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828\]

This constant is as important in mathematics as \(\pi\).

19.3 — Interest-ing Problems

Finding the Interest Rate

Set up the compound interest formula and solve for \(r\).

Example: Sayeed borrows \(\$2{,}300\) compounded annually for 5 years and owes \(\$3{,}301.95\). What is the rate?

\(2300\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^5 = 3301.95\)
\(\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^5 \approx 1.436\)
Take the 5th root: \(1 + \frac{r}{100} \approx 1.075\)
So \(r \approx 7.5\%\).

Present Value

The present value of a future amount is what you'd need to invest today to have that amount later. To find future value you multiply by a growth factor; to find present value you divide by it.

Present Value: The present value of \(\$m\) paid \(n\) years from now at annual compound rate \(r\%\) is: \[\text{Present value} = \frac{\$m}{\left(1 + \frac{r}{100}\right)^n}\]
Example: To have \(\$1{,}000{,}000\) in 10 years at 10% compounded semi-annually:
\(\frac{\$1{,}000{,}000}{(1.05)^{20}} \approx \$376{,}889.48\) — invest this amount today.

Equal Loan Payments

Think of a multi-year loan as several smaller loans, one per payment. The present values of all payments must add up to the total borrowed.

Example: A 3-year, \(\$10{,}000\) loan at 6% compounded annually with equal payments of \(\$x\):
\[\frac{x}{1.06} + \frac{x}{1.06^2} + \frac{x}{1.06^3} = 10{,}000\] Solving gives \(x \approx \$3{,}741.10\) per year.

19.4 — What is a Logarithm?

Definition

A logarithm answers: "What power must I raise the base to in order to get this number?"

The fundamental equivalence (requires \(a > 0\) and \(a \neq 1\)): \[\log_a b = c \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad a^c = b\] The left side is logarithmic form. The right side is exponential form.
Converting between these two forms is the single most important skill in this section.

Key Identity

\[\log_a a^p = p\] "What power do I raise \(a\) to in order to get \(a^p\)?" — obviously \(p\).

Key facts about \(f(x) = \log_3 x\):
Domain: all positive numbers (\(x > 0\); can't take the log of 0 or a negative number)
Range: all real numbers
\(x\)-intercept: always \((1, 0)\) because \(\log_a 1 = 0\) for any valid base
Inverse: \(f(x) = \log_3 x\) and \(g(x) = 3^x\) are inverse functions — their graphs are mirror images over the line \(y = x\).

Common and Natural Logarithms

  • Common logarithm (\(\log_{10}\)): Scientists often write just "\(\log\)" to mean base 10.
  • Natural logarithm (\(\log_e\)): Mathematicians write "\(\ln\)" for base \(e\).

When you see "\(\log\)" without a base, check context to know if it means base 10 or base \(e\).

Solving Logarithmic Equations

The main technique: convert to exponential form.

Example: Evaluate \(\log_{3\sqrt{3}}\!\left(\frac{1}{81}\right)\).

Let \(x = \log_{3\sqrt{3}}\!\left(\frac{1}{81}\right)\), so \((3\sqrt{3})^x = \frac{1}{81}\).
Rewrite: \((3^{3/2})^x = 3^{-4}\), so \(3^{3x/2} = 3^{-4}\).
Match exponents: \(\frac{3x}{2} = -4\), giving \(x = -\frac{8}{3}\).
Example: If \(3 = k \cdot 2^r\) and \(15 = k \cdot 4^r\), find \(r\).

Divide the equations: \(\frac{15}{3} = \frac{4^r}{2^r} = 2^r\), so \(2^r = 5\).
Therefore \(r = \log_2 5\). (This can't be simplified further, and that's okay — logarithms are numbers.)

Useful Property (Previewed)

\[\log_a b^c = c \log_a b\] You can pull exponents out in front of logarithms. You'll prove this in Exercise 19.4.10.

Problem-Solving Strategies

  1. Substitution can turn complicated exponential equations into simpler forms (often quadratics).
  2. Compound interest = multiplication. Each period, multiply by a growth factor. Simple interest = addition.
  3. Present value lets you compare payments made at different times by converting them all to today's dollars.
  4. Converting between log and exponential form is the key to solving most log/exponent problems.
  5. Match bases whenever possible — rewrite all constants with the same base, then equate exponents.
  6. Multiply/divide entire equations (not just add/subtract) to eliminate variables in systems.

Self-Check Questions

Try each one before revealing the answer.

Q1. Solve \(5^{2r-3} = 25\).
Show answer
\(25 = 5^2\), so \(2r - 3 = 2\), giving \(r = 5/2\).
Q2. You invest \(\$10{,}000\) at 10% simple interest for 5 years. How much do you have?
Show answer
\(\left(1 + 5 \times 0.10\right)(\$10{,}000) = 1.5 \times \$10{,}000 = \$15{,}000\).
Q3. You invest \(\$10{,}000\) at 10% compounded annually for 5 years. How much do you have?
Show answer
\((1.10)^5(\$10{,}000) \approx \$16{,}105.10\).
Q4. Evaluate \(\log_4 32\).
Show answer
\(4 = 2^2\) and \(32 = 2^5\), so \(\log_4 32 = \frac{5}{2}\).
Q5. Find all solutions to \(2^{2x} - 8 \cdot 2^x + 12 = 0\).
Show answer
Let \(y = 2^x\). Then \(y^2 - 8y + 12 = 0\), so \((y-2)(y-6) = 0\).
\(y = 2 \Rightarrow x = 1\), or \(y = 6 \Rightarrow x = \log_2 6\).
Q6. What is the present value of \(\$50{,}000\) paid 10 years from now at 8% compounded annually?
Show answer
\(\dfrac{\$50{,}000}{(1.08)^{10}} \approx \$23{,}159.67\).
